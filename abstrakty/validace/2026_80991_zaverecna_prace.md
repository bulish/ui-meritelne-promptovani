Food security remains an important issue in academic research and public policy. One of its basic dimensions is whether national food availability can be maintained under continuous population growth. This thesis examines the relationship between population growth and national food supply in China, Egypt, and Nigeria through a comparative descriptive analysis.
This study uses secondary national-level data from the World Bank and FAOSTAT and focuses on two indicators: population and food supply (kcal/capita/day). China during 1961–1980 is used as the main historical reference period, post-1980 China as supplementary evidence of later improvement, and Egypt and Nigeria during 2010–2023 as recent comparative cases. The benchmark of 2,100 kcal/capita/day is used only as a simplified descriptive reference point.
The findings show that China during 1961–1980 experienced rapid population growth together with relatively limited and uneven food supply, which remained below the benchmark for most of the period and rose slightly above it only in 1980. After 1980, China showed a different pattern, with slower population growth and clearer long-term improvement in food supply. In Egypt and Nigeria, population continued to grow during 2010–2023 without corresponding improvement in food supply. In Egypt, food supply declined overall although it remained above the benchmark. In Nigeria, population growth was stronger, while food supply also ended lower than at the beginning of the period.
Within the limits of this study, the results suggest that continued population growth combined with weak food supply performance may indicate growing food-related pressure. China is not treated as a direct model for Egypt or Nigeria, but its earlier experience is used as a historical reference for understanding why recent trends in Egypt and Nigeria deserve attention.
