This study looks into the shifting forms of territorial control and governance in Myanmar after the 2021 military coup. When the previous administration was removed, the nation witnessed many public demonstrations, which turned into a violent struggle involving the military regime, ethnic armed organizations (EAOs), and People’s Defence Forces (PDFs). Myanmar has started a time of growing territorial fragmentation because the central state is not the only holder of power, and the country now faces a situation where territorial control is shared by different groups, and reshaped by old and new armed actors. The study uses a qualitative research approach and a comparative case study design that looks at four specific, semi-independent regions: Kachin, Kayin, Rakhine, and Sagaing. These regions were picked because they allow to show various modes of territorial control and local governance. Information for this analysis was gathered from secondary sources such as academic literature and data from organizations like ACLED and ISP Myanmar. The results indicate that territorial control in Myanmar changes greatly from one place to another, with implications for society, governance and more. In Kachin, a centralized ethnic armed organization keeps a firm structure of power. In Kayin, the simultaneous presence of several different armed groups has resulted in a fragmented system of management. In Rakhine, the Arakan Army has built a very strong system for territorial control and independent governance. In Sagaing, the resistance is seen as being very scattered because many local groups are currently at work without much connection. Arguably, the local situation determines how the power is used. This paper states that the conflict in Myanmar after the coup is complex and multifaceted The situation shows different levels of division, which are caused by the ability of groups and the local reality. While some regions show the start of authority that looks like a state, other locations remain without peace. Territorial control in Myanmar is affected by how these groups organize themselves, all of which helps us understand how civil wars change the power of a country.
