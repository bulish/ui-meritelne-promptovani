This thesis investigates work–study–life balance among university students in South Korea and in the Czech Republic. The primary objective is to determine how the demands of academic responsibilities, employment, and personal life shape students’ overall wellbeing and overall satisfaction. The study evaluates cross-group differences between the two populations. The theoretical part outlines the development of work–life balance and its relevance for students combining academic, work, and personal roles. It summarizes the main determinants influencing work–study–life balance and conceptualizes well-being through hedonic and eudaimonic perspectives. Furthermore, the methodology and collected data are presented in detail. The empirical part is mainly based on data analysis, using quantitative methods. Finally, the key results of the study are presented and interpreted. According to the findings, Czech and Korean university students differ in some areas how they experience and manage study, work, and personal life demands. Korean students reported higher study time, academic stress, and stress related to social expectations and future uncertainty, while Czech students reported higher engagement in paid work and work-related stress. Czech students also showed higher emotional exhaustion and stronger strain in health and relationships, whereas Korean students reported higher anxiety and overload. Czech students expressed higher satisfaction with work–study–life balance.
