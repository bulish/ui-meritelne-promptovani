In recent years, Thailand has experienced a renewed wave of youth-led political mobilization, particularly during the 2020–2021 pro-democracy protests, which challenged both the military-backed government and long-standing political taboos. This thesis examines the role of student movements in these protests, focusing on how they mobilized, formulated their demands, and interacted with the political environment. The theoretical part introduces key approaches of Social Movement Theory, including resource mobilization, political opportunity structures, and framing, and applies them to a Southeast Asian setting, while also outlining the political and social context of Thailand and briefly introducing the student movements to clarify their main features and significance. The analytical section builds on this foundation and examines the movements in greater depth, focusing on protest dynamics, governmental responses, and their broader political impacts. The results show that student movements mobilized through a combination of digital communication, decentralized coordination, and the use of culturally relatable symbols and references, supported by additional resources such as educational institutions, diverse skill sets, and small-scale funding. Their demands were articulated through coherent framing processes, which enabled them to connect with different social groups and broaden public debate. At the same time, the Thai government responded primarily through legal and repressive measures, including the use of emergency powers, legal prosecution, and restrictions on public assembly, without introducing any institutional reforms. Despite these constraints, the protest movements had impact on political discourse, contributed to increased political polarization, and influenced electoral politics, particularly in the 2023 general election.
