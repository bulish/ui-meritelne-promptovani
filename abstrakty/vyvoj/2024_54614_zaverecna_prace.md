This dissertation describes and examines various historical and traditional methods of wood conservation, emphasizing the use of natural elements and techniques. The dissertation highlights the importance of air, water, fire, and soil in wood conservation and provides a detailed evaluation of these methods. Air drying, one of the oldest techniques, significantly affects the quality of wood by reducing its moisture content and preventing rot. Storing wood in water, another historical method, prevents the growth of fungi and mold by displacing oxygen. Charring, or the application of fire to the wood surface, has historically been used to increase wood's resistance to pests and rot. This method's effects on surface properties, such as color change, surface roughness, charring depth, contact angle measurements, fire and decay resistance, among others, were analyzed. Studies have shown that charring effectively increases the durability and environmental resistance of wood. The dissertation also addresses more advanced historical techniques, including ammonia fumigation and the use of natural substances like plant extracts, animal products, and blood for wood conservation. The effect of gaseous ammonia on oak wood was studied in detail, particularly concerning color change and stability under accelerated aging conditions. Color changes were measured using CIELAB parameters, and the total content of extractives and phenolic substances was measured to determine their impact on this technique. As expected, the results showed that samples treated with gaseous ammonia underwent photodegradation, and a correlation was found between the content of extractive substances and the overall color change. Furthermore, the behavior of historical coatings containing linseed oil with resin additives in the form of glaze and opaque coatings during natural weathering over six years was examined in detail. Chemical changes in linseed oil were evaluated, changes in color, contact angle, and gloss were measured. Although gloss loss, contact angle reduction, and ATR-FTIR analysis indicated chemical changes as expected, the results demonstrated that opaque oil paint coatings exhibited minimal color changes even after six years. By understanding the principles and effectiveness of traditional methods, the work aims to highlight sustainable, eco-friendly solutions for wood conservation. These methods can potentially reduce reliance on harmful synthetic chemicals, aligning with current environmental sustainability goals.
