Kenya’s pursuit of sustainable development is deeply intertwined with the concept of human security, a multidimensional framework that prioritizes the safety, dignity, and well-being of individuals. This thesis explores how human security serves not merely as a development outcome but as a foundational prerequisite for achieving long-term, inclusive progress. In contrast to traditional state-centric security models, human security emphasizes protection from chronic threats such as poverty, disease, environmental degradation, and political instability. These threats are particularly acute in Kenya, where regional disparities, climate vulnerability, and socio- political tensions continue to undermine development efforts.
Drawing on the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) framework and Kenya’s Vision 2030, this study argues that embedding human security into national development strategies enhances resilience, equity, and peace. It examines how targeted interventions in health, education, food systems, and environmental protection can reduce vulnerability and empower communities. Case studies from Northern Kenya and informal settlements in Nairobi illustrate how localized responses to insecurity such as community policing, climate adaptation, and youth empowerment contribute to broader development goals.
The thesis also highlights the importance of institutional capacity, participatory governance, and cross-sectoral collaboration in operationalizing human security. Without addressing the root causes of insecurity, development initiatives risk being unsustainable and exclusionary. Ultimately, the study calls for a paradigm shift in Kenya’s development planning, one that places human security at the core of policy, practice, and progress toward the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). By securing the freedoms and safety of individuals, Kenya can build a more just, peaceful, and prosperous society.
