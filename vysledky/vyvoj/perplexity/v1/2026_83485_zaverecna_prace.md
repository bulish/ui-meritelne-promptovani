Summary
Goal: The study examines the day-to-day experiences, challenges, coping mechanisms, and support systems of LGBTQ community members in Zimbabwe, where same-sex relationships are criminalized.

Methods: The research used a qualitative, critical interpretive design combining thematic analysis and narrative inquiry. It included 10 LGBTQ participants aged 18 years and older, recruited through snowball sampling with initial referrals from GALZ and the Society of Sexual Minorities. Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews, mostly by WhatsApp voice call and some via Microsoft Teams, and analyzed thematically.

Main results: Participants reported family rejection, homelessness, workplace discrimination, fear of termination, blackmail, extortion, police harassment, religious condemnation, and misinformation. Gay men described heightened vulnerability to arrest, extortion, and violence because consensual sodomy is criminalized under Sections 73 and 78 of the Criminal Law (Codification and Reform) Act. Transgender participants reported difficulties changing identity documents, barriers to travel, and limited access to gender-affirming care. Concealment was the most prevalent coping strategy, while participants also used selective disclosure, private online groups, peer support, and self-acceptance. The findings align with Minority Stress Theory: discrimination, victimization, and social prejudice produced emotional distress, anxiety, depression, substance use, and suicidal ideation.

Conclusions: The authors conclude that LGBTQ Zimbabweans experience persistent structural and interpersonal marginalization but also demonstrate resilience. They recommend decriminalization, legal recognition and protection, inclusive workplaces, gender-affirming healthcare, public education, and stronger community support.
