Goal
To map the role of student movements in Thailand’s 2020–2021 pro-democracy protests, examining mobilization, demands, governmental responses, political discourse, and longer-term political influence.

Methods
This qualitative interpretative case study analyzes the 2020–2021 student movement through Social Movement Theory, systematically coding secondary academic literature, international and Thai media, NGO documents, and human-rights reports using resource mobilization, political opportunity structure, and framing.

Main results
The dissolution of the Future Forward Party in February 2020 and dissatisfaction with the 2019 election triggered mobilization in a largely closed political system. Decentralized networks used campuses, Twitter, Facebook, Telegram, LINE, crowdfunding, and creative protest forms; a Free Youth Telegram group reached 200,000 subscribers, while FYM helped organize more than a dozen large-scale protests between July and December 2020 involving hundreds of thousands across Thailand. Protesters demanded the prime minister’s resignation, dissolution of parliament, a new constitution, and later monarchy reform through a ten-point manifesto. Authorities used Article 112, emergency decrees, arrests, surveillance, censorship, water cannons, tear gas, and rubber bullets. In 2023, the Move Forward Party won 151 out of 500 seats; voter participation exceeded 75%, with nearly 40 million votes and around five million first-time voters.

Conclusions
Student movements expanded debate on monarchy reform and influenced electoral politics, but repression and entrenched institutions constrained durable institutional change.
