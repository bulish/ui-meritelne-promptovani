Goal
To define and quantify digital sovereignty in a European value context, classify states’ positions, and identify fields of action for increasing digital sovereignty.

Methods
The dissertation combined structured interviews with five experts and a composite Digital Sovereignty Index (DSI) based on 30 secondary-data parameters. The DSI covers state sovereignty, Digitalisation—focus on KETs, and Digitalisation—technological sovereignty; it was analysed across states, including EU-27 countries, and compared with existing country indices.

Main results
The selected DSI specification uses equally weighted raw data with mean imputation. In the global DSI ranking, the USA ranks 1, Ireland 2, Norway 3, Luxembourg 4, and Switzerland 5. All EU-27 countries are in the TOP-50; Ireland is first among them at world rank 2, while Bulgaria is last among them at world rank 50. Most (21) EU states belong to Category III, the top-performer category. The DSI was robust when each parameter was stochastically perturbed by 20 over 1,000 simulations, particularly for higher-ranked countries. The DSI showed a coefficient of determination of 63 with the European Sovereignty Index; the technology categories showed 73. Interviews attributed the EU’s weak digital-sovereignty position to underestimating technological dependence, limited technological foresight, and insufficiently consistent policy.

Conclusions
Digital sovereignty should not mean digital autarky, but strategic autonomy and the capacity to act in global technological exchange. The author recommends coordinated EU digital market and industrial policy, stronger technology and skills investment, and use of collective market and purchasing power. The DSI is presented as a developable model, constrained by parameter selection, imputation, and its European liberal-democratic framing.
