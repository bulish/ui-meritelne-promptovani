Goal: This thesis assesses whether taxation supports economic growth in Ghana and examines the relationships of capital expenditure, revenue expenditure, and inflation with GDP growth.

Methods: The study analyses annual Ghanaian time-series data from 1990–2022, sourced from the Ghana Revenue Authority, Ministry of Finance and Economic Planning, and World Development Indicators. It models real GDP growth using total tax revenue, capital expenditure, revenue expenditure, and inflation within a multivariate time-series/VECM framework.

Main results: All variables were non-stationary at levels but stationary after first differencing, indicating I(1) series. The selected VAR lag length was 3. Johansen tests rejected ranks 0–3 but not rank 4, indicating cointegration. The reported long-run equation was: GDP = −0.122(inflation) + 0.205(capital expenditure) −0.091(revenue expenditure) + 0.020(total tax revenue) −163. In the GDP VECM, lagged total tax revenue was positive and significant (0.079; p = 0.016); lagged inflation (−0.005; p = 0.402), capital expenditure (0.013; p = 0.682), and revenue expenditure (0.020; p = 0.615) were not significant. The error-correction term was −0.043 (p = 0.023), indicating 4.3% annual adjustment. Total tax revenue Granger-caused GDP (F = 3.905; p = 0.037).

Conclusions: The thesis concludes that tax revenue has a positive causal link with Ghanaian growth. It recommends expanding the tax base, modernising administration, prioritising capital expenditure, and managing the public-sector wage bill.
