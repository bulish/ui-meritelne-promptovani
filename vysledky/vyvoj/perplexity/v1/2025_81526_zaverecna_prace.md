Goal
To examine how diverse actors in Zambian development projects engage with foreign assistance, specifically their motivations for participation, preferred forms of support, and strategies for securing external assistance.

Methods
The study used a qualitative ethnographic approach during an academic field trip to Zambia in autumn of 2024. It drew on 37 informants (11 males and 26 females) in Mongu, Kashitu, and Livingstone, including farmers, cooperative members, NGO actors, and project managers. Data from four focus groups and twenty-seven individual interviews were categorized by requested support and support-seeking strategies, then analysed using thematic analysis.

Main results
Informants sought assistance primarily to increase income, establish or expand small businesses, meet household needs, obtain education or training, and manage climate-related livelihood pressures. Requests emphasized tangible assistance—agricultural equipment, pesticides, sprayers, irrigation pumps, production machinery, marketing support, and training—rather than unrestricted finance. Some participants also sought value-chain inclusion, including honey processing skills and market access. Local actors actively framed needs using donor-oriented concepts such as “empowerment,” “sustainability,” and the Sustainable Development Goals, alongside narratives of water shortages, climate change, continuity, and partnership. Dependency varied within communities: some participants expected continued material assistance, while others were considered able to continue without it.

Conclusions
The thesis concludes that assistance relationships combine local agency, strategic adaptation to donor expectations, and uneven dependency. It argues that development initiatives should centre recipient perspectives and local ownership, and should build autonomy and capacity rather than assume recipients are uniformly dependent or self-sufficient.
