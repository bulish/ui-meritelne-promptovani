Goal
To examine historical wood-preservation methods and scientifically assess selected treatments—surface charring, ammonia-based colour modification, and linseed-oil coatings—as alternatives to industrial chemical preservation.

Methods
The thesis combined a literature review of historical preservation practices with experimental studies of one-sided contact charring of beech, ammonia-gas and iron-nanoparticle treatment of oak during accelerated ageing, and 6-year outdoor weathering of linseed-oil/resin paint systems.

Radial specimens became smoother with increasing temperature, whereas tangential specimens showed less consistent responses and cracking. In the density/wettability study, only radial charred groups showed a statistically significant decrease in water uptake; tangential groups did not. Oak darkened after both ammonia and iron-nanoparticle treatments; ammonia-treated wood had the best colour stability in dry ageing conditions. In the coating study, lazure applications substantially degraded within three years, while opaque coatings retained a continuous layer and showed minimal colour changes after 6 years.

Conclusions
The thesis concludes that historical treatments can provide targeted protective or aesthetic effects, but performance depends strongly on treatment conditions, wood anatomy, and exposure. One-sided charring and opaque linseed-oil coatings show practical promise, while further work is needed to stabilise charred surfaces.
