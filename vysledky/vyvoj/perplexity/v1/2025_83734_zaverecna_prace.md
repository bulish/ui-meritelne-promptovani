Goal
To examine how university students in South Korea and the Czech Republic perceive and experience the balance between academic demands, work responsibilities, and personal life, and how this balance relates to their well-being.

Methods
A quantitative, comparative cross-sectional survey was conducted with employed students at Pukyong National University (South Korea) and Mendel University in Brno (Czech Republic) between June 2024 and January 2025. Data were collected via an online questionnaire and analyzed using descriptive statistics, independent-samples t tests, Spearman correlations, multiple regression, and chi-square tests.

Main results
Korean students spent more time studying (mean 6.28 h/day) than Czech students (4.26 h/day), whereas Czech students spent more time working/interning (2.62 h/day vs 1.31 h/day), on personal time, and on household duties; sleep duration did not differ significantly. Korean students reported significantly higher academic stress, social-expectation stress, and future-uncertainty stress; Czech students reported higher work-related stress. Lack of personal time was the strongest predictor of emotional exhaustion in both groups (Czech β=0.495, p<0.001; Korean β=0.343, p=0.006). Korean students reported lower life satisfaction with work–study–life balance (mean 2.39) than Czech students (mean 2.87; t=2.39, p=0.018) and stronger perceptions that academic/career success takes priority over well-being.

Conclusions
Cultural context shapes the sources and consequences of imbalance, but limited personal time is a shared, critical risk factor for emotional exhaustion. The authors recommend institutional and policy measures that protect students’ personal time and provide targeted support—such as flexible scheduling, workload management, and accessible mental-health services—to improve work–study–life balance and well-being.
