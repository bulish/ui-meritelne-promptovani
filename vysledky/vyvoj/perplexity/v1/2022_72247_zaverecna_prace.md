Goal
To assess Israel’s international-law obligations toward Palestinian minors in the West Bank, compare them with documented practice, and evaluate military-justice reforms.

Methods
This case study combines qualitative and quantitative analysis of international legal texts, scholarly literature, institutional reports, and primarily signed affidavits collected by Defense for Children International – Palestine (DCI-P). Most evidence and testimonies used were no older than ten years.

Main results
The thesis identifies nine relevant international legal texts, including the Fourth Geneva Convention, ICCPR, CAT, and UNCRC. It estimates that 500–700 Palestinian minors enter the Israeli military judicial system annually. In 2013, the IDF arrested 654 minors; 465 were indicted, and 80 were offered release on bail until trial. From 2012–2015, DCI-P documented that 97.7% of minors were restrained, 88.3% blindfolded, 66.7% experienced physical violence during arrest, and 15% experienced solitary confinement for an average of 13 days. During the same period, 99.3% pleaded guilty and only 13.5% were released on bail. DCI-P lawyers filed 35 official complaints of ill-treatment and torture, resulting in no indictments.

Conclusions
The thesis concludes that reforms—juvenile courts, amended detention periods, notification forms, and interrogation safeguards—had limited practical effect because of security-offence exceptions, weak implementation, and failure to address abuse before detention.
