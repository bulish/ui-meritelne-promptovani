Goal
To examine whether publicly available cybercrime-cost estimates display patterns consistent with publication bias, especially higher estimates in industry-sponsored and smaller studies.

Methods
The thesis compiled 20 English-language, publicly available academic, government, and industry reports published from 2011 to 2025. It used a simplified meta-analytical, descriptive approach, comparing cost estimates and source types and constructing a funnel plot with logarithmic cost estimates and inverse sample size

Main results
Reported estimates ranged from under $1 million per incident to predictions exceeding $10 trillion annually. Industry reports generally reported higher per-breach or annual costs than academic and government publications. In the illustrative sample, IBM reported $4.24M from 503 observations, Ponemon $4.45M from 509, FBI IC3 $2.10M from 550, Anderson et al. $1.26M from 300, and Kaspersky $3.80M from 430. The funnel plot showed a concentration of high-cost estimates among studies with relatively small sample sizes, particularly industry sources, and an asymmetric distribution consistent with a possible “small-study effect.” The study did not statistically confirm bias.

Conclusions
The thesis concludes that cybercrime-cost reporting may systematically amplify dramatic, commercially aligned estimates. It recommends methodological and funding disclosure, standardized reporting, independent research, and more critical media interpretation.
