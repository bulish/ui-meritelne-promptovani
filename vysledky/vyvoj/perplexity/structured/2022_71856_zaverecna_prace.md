Goal: This master’s thesis assesses taxation as a catalyst for economic growth in Ghana, focusing on the associations of total tax revenue, capital expenditure, revenue expenditure, and inflation with real GDP growth.

Methods: The study uses annual time-series data for 1990–2022 from the Ghana Revenue Authority, Ministry of Finance and Economic Planning, and World Development Indicators. GDP growth is the dependent variable; total tax revenue, capital expenditure, revenue expenditure, and inflation are explanatory variables. The analysis applies VAR lag selection, Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) unit-root tests, Johansen cointegration tests, a Vector Error Correction Model (VECM), Granger causality tests, and a Doornik-Hansen normality test.

Main results: All variables have unit roots at levels but become stationary after first differencing, indicating integration of order I(1). Johansen tests reject ranks 0–3 but not rank 4, supporting cointegration. The long-run equation reports negative coefficients for inflation (−0.122) and revenue expenditure (−0.091), and positive coefficients for capital expenditure (0.205) and total tax revenue (0.020). In the GDP VECM, lagged total tax revenue is positive and significant (0.079; p = 0.016), while the error-correction term is −0.043 (p = 0.023), implying 4.3% annual adjustment toward equilibrium. Total tax revenue Granger-causes GDP (F = 3.905; p = 0.037).

Conclusions: The thesis concludes that tax revenue is positively linked to Ghana’s growth and recommends broadening the tax base, modernising tax administration, prioritising productive capital expenditure, and managing the public-sector wage bill.
