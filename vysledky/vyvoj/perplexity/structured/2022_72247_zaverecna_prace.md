Goal: This diploma thesis analyzes Israel’s obligations under international law toward Palestinian minors in the West Bank and compares those standards with documented practices from the Second Intifada to the present period covered by the study. It also assesses reforms in the Israeli military justice system.

Methods: The author uses a case-study approach combining qualitative and quantitative evidence. Legal texts, scholarly work, NGO reports, and data are reviewed; evidence on conditions is drawn primarily from signed affidavits collected by Defense for Children International – Palestine (DCI-P), correlated with Israeli-authority data and material used by UNICEF and other organizations.

Main results: The thesis identifies relevant standards in nine instruments, including the Fourth Geneva Convention, ICCPR, CAT, and UNCRC. It reports that 500–700 Palestinian minors enter the military judicial system annually. In 2013, the IDF arrested 654 minors, of whom 465 were indicted. DCI-P testimonies from 2012–2015 reported 97.7% restrained, 88.3% blindfolded, 66.7% experiencing physical violence during arrest, and 99.3% pleading guilty. Between 2012 and 2015, 15% reported solitary confinement, averaging 13 days.

Conclusions: Although reforms created a juvenile military court and amended military orders, the thesis concludes that security-offence exceptions, weak implementation, and unaddressed pre-detention abuse limited meaningful protection of Palestinian minors.
