Goal: This bachelor’s thesis analyzes the evolution of the Czech Republic’s foreign policy toward the Srebrenica genocide from 1995 to 2025, examining how domestic political transformations, historical experiences, and international developments influenced Czech diplomatic discourse and actions, and assessing alignment with the European Union’s position. Methods: The study employs a qualitative case study approach, combining literature review, secondary data analysis of official statements (Czech Ministry of Foreign Affairs, UN, EU, ICC), media sources, and three semi-structured interviews (academia, diplomacy, Srebrenica Memorial Center). Field research included visits to the Siege of Sarajevo Museum and Srebrenica Memorial Center–Potočari. A comparative analysis across seven categories evaluates Czech–EU alignment. Main results: Czech policy shifted from cautious engagement in 1995 to explicit recognition of Srebrenica as genocide in 2005, followed by sustained support for commemorations, international criminal justice (ICTY, ICC), Bosnia and Herzegovina’s EU/NATO integration, and the 2024 UN General Assembly resolution (84 votes in favor) establishing July 11 as an international day of remembrance. Influencing factors include Czech human-rights traditions, post-communist identity, presidential leadership (Havel vs. Zeman vs. Pavel), and UN/NATO/EU engagement. Conclusions: Czech foreign policy increasingly converges with EU norms on genocide recognition and transitional justice, though the EU maintains more systematic educational and institutional initiatives. The thesis underscores the role of memory politics in post-communist European diplomacy.
