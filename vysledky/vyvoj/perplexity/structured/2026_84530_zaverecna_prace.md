Goal: This thesis examines the role of student movements in Thailand’s 2020–2021 pro-democracy protests, addressing how they mobilized and formulated demands, how the government responded, and how the movements affected political discourse. It also assesses longer-term influence through the 2023 general election.

Methods: The study uses an interpretative qualitative case study guided by Social Movement Theory (SMT). It systematically codes secondary academic literature, international and Thai media reporting, NGO documents, and human-rights reports through three categories: resource mobilization, political opportunity structure, and framing.

Main results: The dissolution of the Future Forward Party and dissatisfaction with the 2019 election were key mobilizing triggers within a largely closed political system. Students relied on decentralized networks, campuses, digital platforms, creative protest repertoires, and small-scale donations. Facebook, Twitter, Telegram, LINE, and hashtags such as #FreeYouth supported coordination, collective identity, and visibility. Protesters framed military-backed rule, the 2017 Constitution, repression, and monarchy-related issues as structural problems; they demanded the prime minister’s resignation, dissolution of parliament, a new constitution, and monarchy reform. Authorities primarily used arrests, emergency powers, Article 112, surveillance, censorship, water cannons, tear gas, and rubber bullets.

Conclusions: The movement expanded public debate, especially about the monarchy, but institutional change remained constrained. Its agenda nevertheless entered electoral politics: the Move Forward Party won 151 out of 500 seats in 2023, while later institutional constraints and its 2024 dissolution underscored the persistence of Thailand’s political system.
