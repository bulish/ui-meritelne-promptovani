Summary
Goal: This thesis investigates historical, non-industrial wood-preservation methods and experimentally evaluates selected techniques as potential lower-toxicity alternatives. Methods: It reviews preservation by air, water, fire, soil, ammonia fuming, blood, and plant-derived substances. Experimental work examined one-sided contact charring of Fagus sylvatica L. at 200–400 °C; measurements included CIELAB colour, surface roughness, fire-related weight loss, carbohydrate profiles, fungal decay, X-ray density profiles, water uptake, and contact angle. Oak was treated with ammonia gas and iron nanoparticles and subjected to dry and wet accelerated ageing. Linseed-oil paints containing chromium oxide green, dammar, mastic, or copal varnish were weathered outdoors for 6 years. Main results: Charring reduced beech lightness by approximately 50% in radial and 42% in tangential specimens; 250 °C for six minutes gave the best fire-resistance result in both directions. Only radial charred groups showed a statistically significant decrease in water uptake. Ammonia and iron-nanoparticle treatments darkened oak; ammonia-treated wood had the best colour stability in dry conditions. Lazure coatings substantially degraded within three years, whereas opaque coatings showed minimal colour change after 6 years. Conclusions: Traditional approaches can inspire wood protection, but their performance is treatment-, species-, orientation-, and exposure-dependent; natural preservatives also face leaching, heterogeneity, biodegradation, and regulatory constraints.
