Summary
Goal: This dissertation seeks to define digital sovereignty in a European value context, quantify it, classify states, and identify ways to improve it. It rejects digital autarky as the European model, instead treating sovereignty as the ability to retain technological control and freedom of choice within international exchange.

Methods: The author conducted structured interviews with five experts and developed a Digital Sovereignty Index (DSI) from 30 secondary-data parameters. The DSI comprises three sub-indices: state sovereignty in the sense of good governance, Digitalisation—focus on KETs, and Digitalisation—technological sovereignty. The model tested alternative normalisation, imputation, weighting, and aggregation procedures; the selected calculation used equally weighted raw data with mean imputation. Robustness was examined through 1,000 simulations with each parameter stochastically perturbed by 20.

Main results: The DSI ranks the USA first, Ireland second, Norway third, Luxembourg fourth, and Switzerland fifth; all EU-27 countries appear in the TOP-50. The DSI’s correlation with the European Sovereignty Index is 63, including 73 for technology. Expert interviews identify technological dependence, insufficient foresight, and fragmented policy as European weaknesses.

Conclusions: Digital sovereignty is a multi-decade project requiring strategic technology investment, ICT skills, innovation ecosystems, and common EU digital market and industrial policy. The model remains limited by parameter selection, imputation, and its European liberal-democratic assumptions.
