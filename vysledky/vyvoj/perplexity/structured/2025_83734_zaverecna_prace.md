Goal: To examine how university students in South Korea and the Czech Republic perceive and experience the balance between academic demands, work responsibilities, and personal life, and how this balance is associated with their well-being.

Methods: A quantitative, comparative cross-sectional design was employed. Data were collected via an online questionnaire (five-point Likert scales) from employed students at Pukyong National University (South Korea; analytical sample n≈91) and Mendel University in Brno (Czech Republic; analytical sample n≈88). Analyses included descriptive statistics, independent-samples t tests, Spearman correlations, multiple regression, and chi-square tests.

Main results: Korean students spent more time studying (mean 6.28 h/day) than Czech students (4.26 h/day), whereas Czech students spent more time working/interning (2.62 h/day vs 1.31 h/day), on personal time, and on household duties; sleep duration did not differ significantly. Korean students reported significantly higher academic stress, social-expectation stress, and future-uncertainty stress; Czech students reported higher work-related stress. Lack of personal time was the strongest predictor of emotional exhaustion in both groups (Czech β=0.495, p<0.001; Korean β=0.343, p=0.006). Korean students reported lower life satisfaction with work–study–life balance and stronger perceptions that academic/career success takes priority over well-being.

Conclusions: Cultural context shapes the sources and consequences of imbalance, but limited personal time is a shared, critical risk factor for emotional exhaustion across both countries.
