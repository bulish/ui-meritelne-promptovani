Goal: This diploma thesis investigates whether published estimates of cybercrime-related costs show patterns consistent with publication bias, particularly whether industry-sponsored reports and smaller studies report higher estimates. It does not seek to establish causality or identify a single “true” cybercrime cost.

Methods: The author manually compiled a dataset of 20 publicly available academic, government, and industry studies published from 2011 to 2025. Eligible reports were in English, reported a numerical cybercrime-cost estimate, described a sample size or calculation method, and provided original data. Seven variables were recorded, including publication type, sample size, effect value converted to U.S. dollars, effect type, and bias_proxy = 1/sample_size. A simplified funnel plot used logarithms of cost estimates and inverse sample size; descriptive comparisons supplemented the visual analysis.

Main results: Estimates ranged from under $1 million per incident to predictions exceeding $10 trillion annually. Industry reports tended to report higher costs than academic and government sources. The funnel plot showed high-cost estimates concentrated among relatively small industry studies and an asymmetric distribution consistent with a possible “small-study effect.”

Conclusions: The findings are suggestive rather than conclusive because of the small, heterogeneous sample and visual, non-inferential method. The thesis recommends transparent methodologies, funding disclosure, standardized cost definitions, independent research, and more critical media reporting.
