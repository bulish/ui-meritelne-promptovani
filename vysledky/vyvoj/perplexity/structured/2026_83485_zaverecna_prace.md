Summary
Goal: The study investigates the personal, day-to-day experiences of LGBTQ community members in Zimbabwe, focusing on discrimination, stigma, legal and political pressures, access to services, mental health, coping strategies, and support systems.

Methods: It used a qualitative, critical interpretive design combining thematic analysis and narrative inquiry. The researcher recruited 10 LGBTQ participants aged 18 years and older through snowball sampling, beginning with contacts from GALZ and the Society of Sexual Minorities. Data were collected through in-depth one-on-one interviews, mostly via WhatsApp voice calls and some Microsoft Teams meetings; interviews lasted approximately 15–30 minutes and followed a semi-structured interview guide. Participants provided informed consent, and pseudonyms, anonymization, secure storage, and voluntary withdrawal procedures protected confidentiality.

Main results: Participants reported family rejection, homelessness, workplace discrimination, blackmail, extortion, police harassment, religious condemnation, misinformation, and barriers to healthcare. The study found that criminalization, political homophobia, and cultural/religious norms amplified stigma and distress, consistent with Minority Stress Theory. Participants also used concealment, peer support, digital platforms, community organizations, and self-acceptance as resilience strategies.

Conclusions: The article concludes that LGBTQ Zimbabweans face persistent structural and interpersonal exclusion, but demonstrate agency and resilience. It calls for decriminalization, legal protection, inclusive healthcare—including gender-affirming services—public education, and stronger anti-discrimination protections.
