Summary (184 words)
Goal: This thesis investigates how diverse actors in Zambian development projects engage with foreign assistance: their motivations for participation, the forms of assistance they seek, and the strategies they use to secure support. It also examines whether these relationships indicate dependency.

Methods: The study uses a qualitative ethnographic approach. Data were collected during an academic field trip to Zambia in autumn 2024 through four focus groups and twenty-seven individual interviews, supplemented by participant observation and document analysis. The sample comprised 37 informants—11 males and 26 females—from Mongu, Kashitu, and Livingstone. Responses were categorized and examined through thematic analysis.

Main results: Participants commonly sought income growth, entrepreneurship, household welfare, education, empowerment, and assistance in responding to climate-related livelihood pressures. They preferred practical support—agricultural equipment, irrigation pumps, machinery, market access, training, and value-chain inclusion—over unrestricted financial assistance. Informants actively adapted requests to perceived donor priorities, employing terms such as “empowerment,” “sustainability,” and the SDGs; they also invoked vulnerability, long-term partnership, and prior collaboration. Dependency was heterogeneous: some participants expected continuing material support, whereas others viewed knowledge and technical inputs as routes toward self-sufficiency.

Conclusions: The study characterizes development assistance as a relationship combining need, local agency, strategic donor-oriented communication, and uneven dependency. Assistance designs should support local autonomy rather than presume either passive dependence or uniform capacity for independence.
