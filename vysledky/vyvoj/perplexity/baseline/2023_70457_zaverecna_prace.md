Summary
This thesis develops a more reliable vibro-acoustic framework for evaluating wood and wood-based materials intended for musical instruments. It focuses on acoustic conversion efficiency (ACE), which combines radiation capacity and vibrational damping, and examines how its determination is affected by specimen dimensions, material condition, vibration mode, sensor type, and testing method. Experiments used the frequency resonance technique (FRT), dynamic mechanical analysis, static and impact bending, and finite-element modal analysis on several European wood species, plywood, and modified materials.

The results show that specimen geometry—especially slenderness and cross-sectional shape—can influence measured properties more strongly than specimen size. Moisture above the fibre-saturation point had little effect on dynamic modulus and FRT damping, although DMA damping continued to increase. Fungal degradation of beech sharply reduced dynamic stiffness and increased damping, demonstrating the usefulness of FRT for detecting damage. Thermal modification altered mechanical and impact-related properties, with effects depending on species and treatment severity. Dynamic parameters from different vibration modes or testing methods cannot generally be treated as interchangeable. Finally, combining longitudinal, bending, and torsional measurements into multimodal ACE descriptors provided a more comprehensive distinction between materials than single-mode ACE.
