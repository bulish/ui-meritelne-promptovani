Summary
This diploma thesis examines how internally displaced people (IDPs) from northern Ethiopia use smartphones after conflict-related migration. The study addresses three questions: why migrants use smartphones, which applications they prefer, and how usage varies across demographic and migration-related characteristics. In 2022, the researcher conducted one-to-one interviews with 300 adults in Addis Ababa and towns in the Amhara region, using chain-referral sampling and questionnaires administered in Amharic and Tigrigna. Descriptive statistics, correlations, and machine-learning models were applied to the data.

Communication was the dominant smartphone function: 98.67% of respondents used phones for calls or SMS, while internet access, photography, music, and video were also common. Usage had increased after displacement for 68.33% of participants, and 71.33% considered smartphones helpful in their current situation. Seventy-two percent had at least one social-media account; Facebook and Telegram were the most popular platforms, mainly used to contact family and friends and obtain information. Mobile banking also supported money transfers. However, unreliable internet, limited electricity for charging, and device costs restricted access. Decision-tree and random-forest models achieved approximately 88–89% accuracy, although the author recommends larger, more diverse samples to improve generalizability.
