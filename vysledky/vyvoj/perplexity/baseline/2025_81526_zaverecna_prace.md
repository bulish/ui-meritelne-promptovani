Summary
This thesis examines how local actors in Zambia engage with foreign-funded development projects, focusing on their motivations, preferred forms of assistance, and strategies for obtaining external support. Based on an ethnographic qualitative study involving 37 informants, four focus groups, semi-structured interviews, observations, and document analysis, it finds that participation is driven mainly by the pursuit of higher incomes, household welfare, education, empowerment, and opportunities for entrepreneurship. Some participants also join through peer influence or because development projects have become normalized responses to local hardship.

Rather than prioritizing unrestricted financial aid, participants generally request tangible support: agricultural equipment, irrigation systems, production machinery, market access, technical training, and help entering value chains. Such assistance is valued when it can generate longer-term economic independence, although expectations of continued donor support remain common.

The study also shows that recipients are not passive beneficiaries. They strategically frame their needs using donor-oriented concepts such as “empowerment,” “sustainability,” and the Sustainable Development Goals, while presenting vulnerability, climate pressures, and existing relationships as grounds for assistance. Overall, dependency is uneven: some actors remain reliant on donors, whereas others use knowledge and resources to strengthen local autonomy. The findings therefore portray development assistance as a contested relationship combining genuine need, local agency, strategic adaptation, and persistent structural dependence.
