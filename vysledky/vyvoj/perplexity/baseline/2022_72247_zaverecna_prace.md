This thesis examines the gap between Israel’s international-law obligations and the treatment of Palestinian children in the West Bank from the Second Intifada to 2022. It first explains the territory’s legal and political context, emphasizing the coexistence of Palestinian and Israeli military systems and the fact that Palestinian minors are tried in military courts while Israeli settlers are subject to civilian juvenile law. It then reviews obligations arising from the Fourth Geneva Convention, the Rome Statute, the Beijing Rules, the ICCPR, the ICESCR, CERD, CAT, the UN Rules for the Protection of Juveniles Deprived of Liberty, and the Convention on the Rights of the Child. Against these standards, the thesis identifies recurring problems in arrest, interrogation, detention, trial, imprisonment, education, family contact, and accountability. Testimonies and NGO and UNICEF data describe night arrests, restraints, violence, limited parental or legal assistance, coercive interrogations, solitary confinement, high guilty-plea and conviction rates, transfers outside the occupied territory, and psychological and educational harm. Although reforms introduced juvenile courts, shorter detention periods, bilingual notifications, and recorded interrogations, the thesis argues that security exceptions, weak implementation, and failure to address pretrial abuse substantially limit their effect.
