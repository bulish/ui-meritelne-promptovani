The thesis compares work–study–life balance among employed university students at Pukyong National University in South Korea and Mendel University in the Czech Republic. Using a quantitative, comparative cross-sectional design, the author collected anonymous online questionnaire responses and analyzed them with descriptive statistics, independent-samples t tests, Spearman correlations, regression, and chi-square tests. The groups allocated their time differently: Korean students devoted significantly more time to studying, whereas Czech students spent more time working or interning, on personal activities, and on household duties; sleep duration was similar. Despite comparable perceptions of available time and recovery, Korean students reported greater academic, social-expectation, and future-related stress, while Czech students reported more work-related stress. Lack of personal time was the strongest correlate of emotional exhaustion in both groups. Czech students experienced greater health- and relationship-related interference from study and work, whereas Korean students reported lower life satisfaction and stronger perceptions that academic or career success takes priority over well-being. Korean students were also less likely to view mental-health services as necessary. Overall, the findings indicate that cultural context shapes the sources and consequences of imbalance, although limited personal time is a shared risk factor.
