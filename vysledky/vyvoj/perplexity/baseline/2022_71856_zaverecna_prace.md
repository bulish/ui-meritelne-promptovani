The thesis investigates whether taxation contributes to economic growth in Ghana and how capital expenditure, revenue expenditure, and inflation relate to GDP growth. It analyses annual data for 1990–2022 from the Ghana Revenue Authority, Ministry of Finance and Economic Planning, and World Development Indicators, using a multivariate time-series approach centred on a Vector Error Correction Model.

All variables were non-stationary at levels but became stationary after first differencing, indicating I(1) processes. A VAR lag length of 3 was selected. Johansen cointegration tests rejected ranks 0–3 but not rank 4, supporting long-run cointegration among GDP, inflation, capital expenditure, revenue expenditure, and total tax revenue. The estimated long-run equation associated inflation (−0.122) and revenue expenditure (−0.091) negatively with GDP, while capital expenditure (0.205) and total tax revenue (0.020) were positively associated. In the GDP VECM, lagged total tax revenue had a positive, statistically significant coefficient of 0.079 (p = 0.016); lagged inflation, capital expenditure, and revenue expenditure were not significant. The error-correction term was −0.043 (p = 0.023), implying a 4.3% annual adjustment toward equilibrium. Total tax revenue Granger-caused GDP (F = 3.905; p = 0.037). The thesis concludes that stronger tax mobilisation and productive capital spending can support Ghana’s growth, recommending a broader tax base, modernised administration, rationalised exemptions, and management of the public-sector wage bill.
