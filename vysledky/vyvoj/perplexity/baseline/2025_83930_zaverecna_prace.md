The thesis examines how the Czech Republic’s foreign policy toward the Srebrenica genocide developed between 1995 and 2025, using qualitative case-study research, document analysis, and interviews. It argues that Czech policy evolved from cautious diplomatic engagement into a clear commitment to recognition, remembrance, accountability, and genocide prevention. In 1995, Czech representative Karel Kovanda condemned the atrocities at the UN Security Council and criticized the international community’s failure to anticipate them. A major shift came in 2005, when the Czech Ministry of Foreign Affairs explicitly recognized Srebrenica as genocide and linked reconciliation to confronting nationalist ideologies and prosecuting perpetrators. Subsequent Czech governments supported commemorations, international criminal justice, Bosnia and Herzegovina’s Euro-Atlantic integration, and the preservation of historical memory. The thesis identifies Czech human-rights traditions, post-communist identity, domestic political leadership, and engagement with the UN, NATO, and EU as key influences. Overall, Czech policy broadly converged with EU positions, particularly on recognition and justice, although the EU’s educational and institutional initiatives remain more extensive. The Czech Republic’s support for the 2024 UN resolution establishing an annual Srebrenica remembrance day represents the culmination of this trajectory.
