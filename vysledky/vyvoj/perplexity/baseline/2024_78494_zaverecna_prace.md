Summary
This thesis examines income inequality across all 27 European Union member states from 2007 to 2023 using the Gini coefficient, Atkinson index, and Generalized Entropy measures. Using Eurostat decile data on national equivalised income, supplemented by Croatian statistics, it compares overall trends and investigates which income groups drove changes. The results show that the EU-wide average remained broadly stable, although this masks substantial national differences. Romania and Poland recorded the largest reductions in inequality, associated with increased income shares among lower deciles and declining shares for the richest group. Slovakia remained the most equal country according to the 2023 Gini coefficient, while Bulgaria and Lithuania consistently displayed high inequality. Sweden and Malta experienced marked increases, driven primarily by gains among top-income groups and losses among lower deciles. The parameter-sensitive measures reveal patterns that the Gini can conceal: Atkinson values emphasize disadvantages among poorer households, whereas Generalized Entropy measures, especially with higher parameters, highlight concentration at the top. The study therefore concludes that combining complementary indicators provides a more complete picture than relying on the Gini alone, and that effective responses require country-specific redistribution, education, and labor-market policies.
