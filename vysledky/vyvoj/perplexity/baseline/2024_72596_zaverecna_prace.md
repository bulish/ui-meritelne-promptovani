Summary
The dissertation develops a European value-based model for defining and measuring state digital sovereignty. It argues that digital sovereignty is not equivalent to technological autarky: a sovereign state should retain freedom of choice and political agency while participating in controlled international technological exchange. The proposed Digital Sovereignty Index (DSI) combines three dimensions: state sovereignty and good governance, attention to key enabling technologies, and technological sovereignty. Thirty indicators drawn from established secondary datasets are normalised, weighted through several approaches, aggregated, and tested through uncertainty, sensitivity, simulation, and comparison with other indices.

Five expert interviews inform the model and suggest that Europe’s weak position results from long-term technological dependence, insufficient foresight, fragmented policies, and inadequate development of digital skills and industries. The analysis identifies substantial differences among European states and groups them according to their relative strengths and weaknesses. Recommended measures include investing in education and ICT expertise, supporting strategic technologies, improving innovation and start-up ecosystems, using public procurement to strengthen European capabilities, and coordinating EU market power. The author stresses that digital sovereignty is a multi-decade project. Results remain provisional because the index depends on selected parameters, imputed data, European democratic assumptions, and linear methods.
