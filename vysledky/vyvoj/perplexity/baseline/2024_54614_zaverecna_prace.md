The dissertation examines historical and natural methods for extending wood’s service life, combining literature research with experiments on charring, ammonia treatment, and traditional oil paints. It emphasizes that wood’s variability makes universal conclusions difficult and that preservation methods must balance durability, environmental impact, appearance, and maintenance. Traditional approaches include air-drying, water storage, charring, burial, ammonia fuming, animal-derived coatings, plant extracts, phenolics, essential oils, fatty acids, and alkaloids. The experimental studies focus especially on surface carbonization, which chemically alters wood and can improve resistance to moisture and biological decay, although its effectiveness depends on wood species, temperature, oxygen availability, and process control. Ammonia fuming changes oak colour and may improve colour stability during weathering, while iron nanoparticles are investigated as an additional treatment. A six-year outdoor study of linseed-oil paints shows that opaque coatings remain comparatively stable, whereas thin lazure coatings deteriorate markedly after about three years. Plant-based preservatives are promising because of their antimicrobial properties, but leaching, inconsistent composition, biodegradation, legislation, and differences between laboratory and field performance remain major obstacles. Overall, the work presents historical techniques as useful sources of ideas rather than straightforward replacements for modern preservatives.
