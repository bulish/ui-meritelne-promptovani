Summary
This thesis examines whether early retirement influences the shadow economy in Austria, Czechia, Germany, Hungary, Poland, Slovakia, and Slovenia. Using annual data from 2010–2024, it applies country-specific MIMIC structural-equation models, treating the shadow economy as an unobserved variable reflected through GDP growth and labour-force participation. The analysis includes retirement age, GDP per capita, unemployment, self-employment, taxation, social-security contributions, government effectiveness, and migration.

The central finding is that retirement age had no statistically significant relationship with the shadow economy in six countries. Hungary was the exception: a higher retirement age for women was associated with a smaller shadow economy, which the thesis links to the Women 40 programme, where only formal employment contributes to pension eligibility. Across countries, other determinants were more important and their effects differed by institutional context. Personal income tax was positively associated with informal activity in Czechia, Hungary, and Slovakia. Social-security contributions were significant in all countries except Slovakia and generally had a negative association with the shadow economy, possibly because formal employment provides valued pension, health, and welfare entitlements. Formalised self-employment also reduced shadow activity in Austria, Germany, and Poland. The authors conclude that pension parameters alone have limited explanatory power and that national institutions, taxation, and social-protection systems are more influential.
