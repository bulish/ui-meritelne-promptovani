This thesis investigates whether publication bias influences publicly available estimates of cybercrime costs. It compiles 20 academic, governmental, and industry reports and examines whether smaller studies and industry-sponsored publications tend to present higher estimates. The analysis identifies patterns consistent with selective visibility. Industry reports generally present higher per-breach or annual cost estimates than academic and government sources, while the most extreme figures are often associated with small or insufficiently transparent samples. The funnel plot appears asymmetric, suggesting a possible small-study effect, although the author emphasizes that this does not establish causality or prove intentional manipulation. The thesis also argues that dramatic figures are amplified through media repetition, commercial incentives, and the uncritical importation of foreign statistics, including in Azerbaijan. It recommends publishing detailed methodologies, disclosing funding, standardizing cost definitions, strengthening independent research, and improving journalists’ statistical literacy. The study’s main limitations are its small, heterogeneous sample and reliance on visual rather than inferential analysis.
