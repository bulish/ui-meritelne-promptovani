The thesis examines the role of student movements in Thailand’s pro-democracy protests during 2020–2021. Using a qualitative case-study design and Social Movement Theory, it analyzes resource mobilization, political opportunity structures, and framing processes. The movement emerged amid dissatisfaction with the 2019 election, the military-backed government, and the Constitutional Court’s dissolution of the Future Forward Party. Students mobilized through decentralized networks, university communities, crowdfunding, creative protest, and digital platforms including Facebook, Twitter, Telegram, and LINE. These tools enabled rapid coordination, solidarity, international visibility, and resilience under repression, although the movement’s loose structure complicated long-term organization and negotiation.

Protesters initially demanded the prime minister’s resignation, parliamentary dissolution, and a new constitution. Their agenda later expanded to monarchy reform, constitutional accountability, and greater transparency. Through democratic, generational, and popular-culture frames—including the three-finger salute—the movement broadened political participation and challenged taboos surrounding the monarchy. The government responded mainly with arrests, emergency powers, prosecutions, censorship, surveillance, and force rather than substantive reform. Although institutional change remained limited, the protests reshaped public discourse, intensified polarization, and influenced the 2023 success of the Move Forward Party, demonstrating lasting electoral and agenda-setting effects.
