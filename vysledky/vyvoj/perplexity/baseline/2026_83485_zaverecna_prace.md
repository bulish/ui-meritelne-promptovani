Summary
This study examines how LGBTQ individuals in Zimbabwe experience discrimination, stigma, and exclusion, while also documenting the strategies they use to survive and resist oppression. It argues that colonial-era criminal laws, particularly provisions penalizing same-sex intimacy, remain intertwined with political rhetoric, religious conservatism, and socially enforced heterosexual norms. Together, these forces produce discrimination in families, workplaces, healthcare, housing, and public life, as well as harassment, violence, extortion, and barriers to HIV-related services. The study applies Minority Stress Theory to explain how external pressures—such as criminalization, prejudice, and violence—interact with internal burdens including concealment and fear, harming mental health and wellbeing. It also emphasizes that LGBTQ experiences are not uniform: gender identity, socioeconomic status, and rural or urban location shape exposure to risk. Rather than portraying LGBTQ people solely as victims, the research highlights agency and resilience through “strategic invisibility,” clandestine support networks, digital communication, art, community organizations, legal advocacy, and transnational alliances. Using qualitative interviews, the study seeks to foreground personal narratives and identify support systems and coping mechanisms, ultimately advocating decriminalization, inclusive policies, and stronger community-based protections.
