## Goal

This thesis analyzed how international law impacts Israel's treatment of Palestinian minors in the West Bank by identifying international legal obligations and comparing them with realities on the ground.

## Methods

The study utilized a case study format combining qualitative and quantitative data—primarily no older than ten years—derived from signed affidavits, non-governmental organization reports (including Defense for Children International - Palestine and Israeli associations), UNICEF bulletins, international lawyers' associations, and scholarly articles.

## Main Results

Between 500 and 700 Palestinian minors annually encounter the Israeli military judicial system. Evidence showed widespread ill-treatment: 60% of minors were arrested between midnight and 5 a.m., 97.7% were restrained and 88.3% blindfolded, and 66.7% experienced physical violence during arrest. Furthermore, 97% of interrogated minors lacked a lawyer or parent present, and 15% faced solitary confinement with an average isolation period of 13 days, resulting in 90.9% of those isolated confessing. High confession rates and extensive pre-trial detention drove military court conviction rates to 99.74% in 2010, while 99.3% pleaded guilty between 2012 and 2015. Additionally, between 2012 and 2015, DCI-P lawyers filed 35 official complaints for ill-treatment and torture, yielding zero indictments.

## Conclusions

The authors concluded that recent military legal reforms are largely tokenistic due to broad security exemption clauses and a persistent gap between policy and practice. They recommended relying on grassroots initiatives and societal change on the Palestinian side to heal trauma and secure future opportunities.
