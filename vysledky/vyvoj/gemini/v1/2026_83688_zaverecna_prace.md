## Goal

The study investigates the potential impact of early retirement and retirement age on the size of the shadow economy across seven Central European countries—Austria, the Czech Republic, Germany, Hungary, Poland, Slovakia, and Slovenia—while assessing associated tax losses and measuring the overall shadow economy from 2010 to 2024.

## Methods

The quantitative analysis utilized secondary macroeconomic panel data from 2010 to 2024 sourced from the World Bank and OECD. The MIMIC (Multiple Indicators Multiple Causes) model was implemented via Stata 19 using structural equation modeling (SEM) and estimated via the Maximum Likelihood method.

## Main Results

Retirement age showed no statistically significant impact on the shadow economy in most countries, except for Hungary, where women's retirement age exhibited a significant negative effect ($\beta = -9.494764, p = 0.041$). Social security contributions (SSC) significantly affected the shadow economy in nearly all countries, demonstrating an inverse relationship in Austria ($\beta = -11.69388, p = 0.029$), Germany ($\beta = -7.860026, p = 0.000$), Czechia ($\beta = -3.700902, p = 0.008$), Poland ($\beta = -3.834507, p = 0.023$), and Slovenia ($\beta = -3.552688, p = 0.021$), but a positive relationship in Hungary ($\beta = 6.706555, p = 0.000$). Personal income tax (PIT) positively drove the shadow economy in Czechia ($\beta = 2.517783, p = 0.000$), Hungary ($\beta = 5.024974, p = 0.000$), and Slovakia ($\beta = 4.825519, p = 0.018$). Self-employment showed an inverse effect in Austria ($\beta = -2.949739, p = 0.047$), Germany ($\beta = -2.398985, p = 0.021$), and Poland ($\beta = -2.894794, p = 0.020$).

## Conclusions

Pension parameters alone are insufficient to alter the shadow economy without complementary institutional changes. The authors recommend adopting a comprehensive policy approach focused on institutional quality and formal-sector incentive structures rather than relying on uniform, isolated parameters like retirement age.
