## Summary of the Research Article

### Goal

This study investigates whether systematic patterns suggesting publication bias exist in publicly available estimates of cybercrime-related costs.

### Methods

The research employs a simplified meta-analytical approach using a manually compiled dataset of 20 publicly available studies drawn from academic, governmental, and private industry sources between 2011 and 2025. It utilizes a funnel plot with the inverse of the sample size ($1/n$) as a proxy for statistical precision to visually analyze the distribution of reported monetary cost estimates.

### Main Results

The analysis reveals an asymmetric distribution consistent with the "small-study effect". Industry-sponsored reports consistently report higher costs—often exceeding $\$4$ million per breach—despite relying on relatively modest or undisclosed sample sizes. For example, the IBM (2021) and Ponemon Institute (2023) reports estimate per-breach costs at $\$4.24$ million (sample size of $503$) and $\$4.45$ million (sample size of $509$), respectively. In contrast, academic studies such as Anderson et al. (2012) present more conservative estimates, such as $€265$ billion per year for the EU with a sample size of $300$. Government sources, such as the FBI IC3 (2020) report, show intermediate values (e.g., $\$2.10$ million based on $550$ cases).

### Conclusions

The authors conclude that cybercrime cost estimates are subject to selective visibility driven by commercial and institutional incentives, leading to the systematic amplification of dramatic figures. They recommend that policymakers approach headline figures with caution, that cybersecurity firms adopt greater methodological transparency, and that researchers and journalists rigorously verify data sources.
