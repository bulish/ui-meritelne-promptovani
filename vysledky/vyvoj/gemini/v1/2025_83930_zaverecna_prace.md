* **Goal:** To analyze the evolution of the Czech Republic's foreign policy regarding the Srebrenica genocide between 1995 and 2025, and to examine how historical, political, and international factors shaped this diplomatic approach and its alignment with the European Union.


* **Methods:** This qualitative case study employed secondary data analysis of official documents, scholarly literature, and media sources from Czech, English, and Bosnian languages. It also incorporated primary field research conducted in Bosnia and Herzegovina in March and April 2025, which included site visits and three semi-structured expert interviews.


* **Main Results:** The Czech Republic's stance transitioned from initial post-1995 uncertainty to official recognition of the Srebrenica massacre as genocide in July 2005. Key quantitative and qualitative milestones include Czechia deploying 800 troops to IFOR in 1996, supporting the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia, contributing a voluntary financial contribution of CZK 1,000,000 (around EUR 40,000) to the International Criminal Court’s Trust Fund for Victims in 2024, and voting in favor of the May 2024 UN General Assembly resolution that established July 11th as the International Day of Reflection and Remembrance for the Srebrenica Genocide. Furthermore, high-level diplomatic visits were conducted, such as Chamber of Deputies President Markéta Pekarová Adamová visiting the Potočari Memorial Center in 2024. While highly aligned with EU positions on transitional justice, Czech educational and institutional frameworks remain more ad hoc compared to structured EU programs like the Genocide Network.


* **Conclusions:** The authors conclude that the Czech Republic's foreign policy on Srebrenica evolved into a principled, values-driven stance rooted in human rights and historical memory, heavily influenced by the legacies of figures like Václav Hargreaves/Havel, democratic transitions, and Western integration. The study recommends that the Czech Republic and other EU states strengthen their engagement with post-conflict societies by investing in education, supporting a culture of remembrance, and actively combating genocide denial.
