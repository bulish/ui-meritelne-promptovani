## Summary

**Goal**
This study investigates how various actors involved in development projects in Zambia perceive, depend on, and strategically navigate foreign development assistance.

**Methods**
The study employed a qualitative ethnographic research design conducted during an academic field trip to Zambia in the autumn of 2024. Data collection relied primarily on 27 semi-structured individual interviews, 4 focus groups, participant observation, and document analysis, involving a purposive and theoretical sample of 37 informants (11 males and 26 females) aged 18 to 77 across Mongu, Kashitu, and Livingstone.

**Main Results**
Informants' primary motivations were individual economic improvement, increased income, and family welfare, with women emphasizing empowerment and informal enterprise development (where the informal sector employs ~90% of the workforce). Participants favored practical and tangible assistance—such as agricultural tools, equipment (e.g., oil-pressing machines, water pumps), pesticides, and market access—over unrestricted funds. Local actors demonstrated active agency by strategically framing requests, using development terminology like "empowerment" and "sustainability," and aligning with global frameworks such as the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) to attract support. While some participants exhibited long-term dependence, project managers noted that others gained self-sufficiency through acquired knowledge.

**Conclusions**
The authors conclude that foreign assistance in Zambia is frequently treated as a structural necessity rather than a temporary tool, reinforcing cycles of aid dependency. They recommend that donors transition away from top-down planning toward equitable, long-term partnerships that foster local autonomy, institutional capacity, and self-sustaining strategies rooted in local ownership.
