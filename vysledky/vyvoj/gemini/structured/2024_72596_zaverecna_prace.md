### 1. Summary

#### Goal

The dissertation aims to make the term "digital sovereignty" more accessible and concrete from the perspective of the European value system, to classify states based on it, and to establish actionable pathways for improvement.

#### Methods

The research design integrates etymological analysis, historical comparison of the four industrial revolutions, and the synthesis of a composite conceptual definition of digital sovereignty. This normative definition is operationalized through a hierarchical quantitative model comprising three sub-indices: state sovereignty (measured via de jure status, good governance, and prosperity), focus on key enabling technologies (KETs), and technological sovereignty. Using secondary data across approximately 200 countries from databases such as the World Bank and OECD, a composite Digital Sovereignty Index (DSI) is constructed via data normalization, imputation, weighting procedures (including Equal Weighting, Principal Component Analysis, and Analytical Hierarchy Process), and linear aggregation. Expert interviews, uncertainty/sensitivity analyses, and validations against existing country indices are employed to verify the model.

#### Main results

The synthesis defines a digitally sovereign state within the European value framework as one where state sovereignty is maintained through "good governance," KET-driven digitalization is defined and widely accepted, and technological sovereignty is established under weak data sovereignty, ensuring technologies can be understood, controlled, or replaced. Data and index evaluations reveal that while the US and China dominate technological scaling, digital market access, start-up investments, and KET patents (such as AI and quantum technologies), EU states face severe dependencies on non-European hardware and software. However, several small European nations demonstrate high relative autonomy supported by robust governance and strategic integration.

#### Conclusions

Digital sovereignty is not achieved through isolationist autarky (as practiced by autocratic states like China or Russia), but through strategic autonomy, freedom of choice, and the capacity to act as an equal negotiating partner. To safeguard European values and state sovereignty, the EU must implement a cohesive, implementation-oriented digital policy focusing on KET co-investments, public procurement optimization, and advanced digital skills training.

### 2. Supporting Passages

* **Goal**: "This dissertation aims to make the term digital sovereignty more accessible from the perspective of the European value system, as well as to concretise the term, to indicate how states can be classified, and to create room for improvement."


* **Methods**: "To this end, an etymological derivation of the terms sovereignty and digitalisation, as well as their classification within a historical context, is first provided... Through synthesis, a composite conceptual definition of digital sovereignty is proposed. The definition is then structured with a qualitative component model and quantified with secondary data."


* **Main Results**: "A state is to be understood as digitally sovereign if its sovereignty is preserved in the sense of the Foucaultian approach of 'good governance' based on European values... the technological digitalisation by means of KETs is defined... and technology sovereignty is established under the aspect of weak data sovereignty so that the technologies used by state organisations are understood and can be controlled or replaced at any time if necessary."


* **Conclusions**: "Here, strategic autonomy means neither setting oneself apart technologically (in the sense of autarky) nor falling into absolute dependence, but becoming aware of global dependencies and actively shaping them in the sense of freedom of choice—'being able to perform and compete in a self-determined manner'."
