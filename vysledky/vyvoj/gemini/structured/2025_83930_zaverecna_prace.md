### Goal

This thesis examines the evolution of the Czech Republic's foreign policy concerning the Srebrenica genocide between 1995 and 2025, analyzing how historical experiences, domestic political transformations, and international developments shaped Czech diplomatic discourse and actions. It also assesses the extent of alignment and divergence between Czech foreign policy and the official positions of the European Union.

### Methods

The study employs a qualitative case study approach utilizing secondary data analysis, drawing on official documents from the Czech Ministry of Foreign Affairs, the United Nations, the European Parliament, and the International Criminal Court, alongside academic literature and media sources. Field research included a two-month internship and visits to the Srebrenica Memorial Center in Potočari and the Siege of Sarajevo Museum. Additionally, the author conducted three semi-structured qualitative interviews with key experts: Prof. Dr. Ermin Kuka (Institute for Research of Crimes Against Humanity and International Law in Sarajevo), Ing. Josef Zrzavecký (Deputy Ambassador of the Czech Republic to Bosnia and Herzegovina), and Azir Osmanović (Director of the Srebrenica Memorial Center).

### Main results

Czech foreign policy evolved from initial hesitation and uncertainty in 1995 toward a strong, consistent commitment to recognition, international justice, and remembrance. A major turning point occurred in 2005 when the Czech Ministry of Foreign Affairs officially recognized the Srebrenica massacre as genocide, describing it as "undoubtedly the worst single atrocity in Europe since the end of World War II". Over the subsequent decades, Czech leadership consistently supported international criminal tribunals (ICTY and ICC), rejected genocide denial, and actively participated in commemorations. Notably, in May 2024, the Czech Republic voted in favor of the UN General Assembly resolution establishing July 11 as the "International Day of Reflection and Remembrance for the 1995 Srebrenica Genocide". Comparison with the EU reveals a high level of policy alignment, though the EU maintains more institutionalized and systematic educational frameworks (such as Erasmus+ and the Genocide Network), whereas Czech efforts remain more individual or ad-hoc.

### Conclusions

The Czech Republic's foreign policy demonstrates that a small, post-communist state can successfully adopt a values-driven, proactive role in global human rights advocacy and transitional justice. Shaped by democratic traditions, historical memories of totalitarian oppression, and leadership figures like Václav Havel, Czechia's diplomatic approach increasingly mirrors European Union norms. Nonetheless, occasional domestic political ambivalence highlights ongoing tensions between national values and internal politics, underscoring the continuous need to combat denial through structured education and remembrance.
