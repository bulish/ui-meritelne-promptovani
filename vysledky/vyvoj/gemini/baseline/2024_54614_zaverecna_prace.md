This dissertation thesis investigates historical and traditional techniques of wood preservation, focusing on methods used prior to the Industrial Revolution and comparing them with modern approaches. The author examines foundational elements—air, water, fire, and soil—detailing processes such as natural air-drying, water storage, surface charring, and subterranean preservation.

Advanced historical methodologies are also evaluated, including ammonia fuming, protein-based coatings (such as cattle blood), plant-derived extractives, and resin-modified linseed oil paints. Through experimental analyses, the research assesses the physical, mechanical, chemical, and aesthetic impacts of these techniques. Key findings demonstrate that one-sided surface charring of beech wood improves fire resistance and color stability depending on temperature and anatomical orientation. Furthermore, treatments involving ammonia gas and iron nanoparticles on oak wood prompt significant dark discoloration strongly correlated with extractive and phenolic content. Finally, outdoor weathering tests on traditional oil-and-resin paint formulations reveal that opaque layers maintain superior color and chemical stability over several years compared to thin lazure applications.
