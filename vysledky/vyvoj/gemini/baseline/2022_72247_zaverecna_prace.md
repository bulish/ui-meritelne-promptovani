Summary
This thesis investigates the status of Palestinian children in the West Bank under Israeli military occupation, contrasting Israel's binding obligations under international law with the reality on the ground. Since the 1967 Six-Day War, a dual legal system has persisted in the West Bank: Israeli settlers are subject to civil law, whereas Palestinian minors are prosecuted within the Israeli military court system.

Drawing on international instruments such as the Fourth Geneva Convention, the Convention on the Rights of the Child, and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, the study highlights widespread discrepancies. Documented testimonies reveal systematic human rights violations during arrests—often conducted at night—along with severe physical abuse, painful restraint methods, lack of notification, absence of legal counsel or parents during interrogations, and forced confessions. Furthermore, the extraterritorial imprisonment of minors in facilities inside Israel and restricted access to education violate international standards. Although recent legislative reforms, such as the establishment of a military juvenile court and amended military orders, aim to improve juvenile protections, critics characterize them as superficial due to broad exception clauses for "security offenses."
