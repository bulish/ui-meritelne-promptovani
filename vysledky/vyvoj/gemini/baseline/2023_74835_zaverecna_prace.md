Summary
This diploma thesis investigates the intersection of smart migration and digital technology among internally displaced persons (IDPs) affected by the conflict in northern Ethiopia. Based on a survey of 300 respondents across Addis Ababa and the Amhara region, the study explores smartphone usage patterns, preferred mobile applications, and the socio-demographic profile of the displaced population.

The findings indicate that smartphones serve as a vital lifeline for IDPs, primarily used for making regular calls/SMS, accessing the internet, taking pictures, and entertainment. Over 72% of participants maintain social media accounts, with Facebook and Telegram emerging as the most popular platforms for communicating with family, obtaining information, and entertainment. Furthermore, 57% of respondents utilize mobile phones for financial transactions such as mobile banking. Despite challenges like poor internet connectivity, device affordability, and power shortages for charging, mobile technology significantly aids migrants in navigation and maintaining social cohesion. Finally, the study successfully implements and compares machine learning models—namely Decision Trees and Random Forests—to predict smartphone usage reasons and application preferences, achieving an accuracy score of approximately 88%.
