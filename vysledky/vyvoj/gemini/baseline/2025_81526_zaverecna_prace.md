This bachelor's thesis investigates the dynamics of development assistance dependency in Zambia through a qualitative, ethnographic lens. Centered on semi-structured interviews and focus groups with local actors—such as farmers, cooperative members, and project managers—the study explores why participants seek foreign aid, what specific resources they look for, and how they strategically frame their appeals.

The findings reveal that local stakeholders are primarily driven by pragmatic needs, including income generation, family welfare, and capacity-building. Rather than seeking open-ended financial transfers, participants predominantly favor tangible and practical support, such as agricultural equipment, tools, market access, and technical training to mitigate climate challenges. Furthermore, the research demonstrates that local actors are not passive beneficiaries; instead, they display active agency by strategically adapting their language, leveraging development discourse, and invoking concepts like sustainability and empowerment to secure continuous external support. Ultimately, the study concludes that while these strategies reflect rational adaptation, they can also reinforce structural aid dependency, underscoring the need for long-term, equitable partnerships that foster genuine local self-sufficiency.
