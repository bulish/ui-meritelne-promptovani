This diploma thesis investigates whether publication bias is present in publicly available estimates of cybercrime costs. Using a manually compiled dataset of 20 studies from academic, governmental, and private industry sources, the author evaluates the relationship between reported cost estimates and sample size.

To detect potential asymmetries and distortions, the research applies a visual meta-analytical tool—the funnel plot—where the inverse of sample size serves as a proxy for statistical precision. The empirical results reveal patterns consistent with the "small-study effect," indicating that smaller, industry-sponsored reports tend to systematically project significantly higher and more dramatic cost estimates compared to more conservative, methodologically transparent academic and government publications.

The study concludes that the current reporting ecosystem is prone to selective visibility and commercial incentives, which can distort public and policy narratives. Consequently, the author recommends greater transparency in data collection, improved statistical literacy among media professionals, and a more critical approach by policymakers when interpreting cybercrime economic reports.
