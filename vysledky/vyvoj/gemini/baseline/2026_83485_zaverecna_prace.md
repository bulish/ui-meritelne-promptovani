This Master's thesis explores the lived experiences of LGBTQ+ community members in Zimbabwe, examining how legal restrictions, cultural norms, and religious conservatism shape their daily lives. Grounded in Minority Stress Theory, the study highlights how institutionalized homophobia and colonial-era penal codes—such as Sections 73 and 78 of the criminal and constitutional frameworks—create pervasive distal and proximal stressors.

Through qualitative interviews with 18 participants, the research reveals that LGBTQ+ individuals face severe discrimination in workplaces, religious institutions, and public spheres, often leading to family disownment, homelessness, and physical or verbal abuse. Transgender and intersex individuals encounter distinct structural barriers, including mismatched legal identity documents and limited access to healthcare. To navigate these hostile environments, participants employ survival strategies such as concealing their identities, selective disclosure, and utilizing digital support networks. Furthermore, community-based organizations like GALZ serve as vital safe spaces providing legal aid and psychosocial support. Ultimately, the thesis underscores a paradox of visibility, where public expression invites state and societal violence, while invisibility reinforces systemic erasure.
