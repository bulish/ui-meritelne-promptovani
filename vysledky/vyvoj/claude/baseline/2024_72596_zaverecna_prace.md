Summary (about 185 words)

This dissertation addresses the lack of a uniform, measurable concept of digital sovereignty, particularly for EU states, whose dependence on non-European ICT hardware and software has grown since the 1980s. Drawing on the history of sovereignty and digitalisation, it proposes a normative definition: a state is digitally sovereign if it preserves “good governance” based on European values, defines its use of key enabling technologies (KETs), and can understand, control or replace the technologies its institutions use. Autocratic autarky is excluded by definition. The author operationalises this with three sub-indices (state sovereignty, KETs, technological sovereignty), ten sub-components and 30 public secondary-data parameters, informed by structured expert interviews. After imputation, normalisation, weighting and aggregation, a Digital Sovereignty Index (DSI) ranks states. The index is validated through correlations with other indices, a stochastic stress test and simulation. The USA, Ireland and Norway lead; all EU-27 states are in the top 50, and the EU as a bloc would rank fourth. Recommendations are tailored to three EU country groups, plus joint EU action. Limitations include reliance on regression with R² ≥ 50%, imputation choices and a Western-democratic bias.
