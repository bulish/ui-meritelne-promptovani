Summary (about 170 words)

This master’s thesis examines the lived experiences of LGBTQ people in Zimbabwe, where a colonial-era law (Section 73) criminalizes male same-sex acts and the constitution bans same-sex marriage. Using minority stress theory, the author interviewed 18 participants aged 18–35 (lesbian, gay, transgender and intersex) by phone and Teams. Recruitment was by snowball sampling through GALZ and PAKASIPITI, and the data were analysed thematically. Participants described stigma rooted in religious and cultural views of homosexuality as “un-African.” They reported job loss, exclusion from women’s football, rejection by churches, family disownment, forced marriage, and blackmail and extortion, including by police. They also reported verbal and sexual violence. Fear of arrest kept many from reporting abuse. Transgender participants faced identity documents that do not match their gender, a refused border crossing, and limited access to hormone therapy. Common coping strategies were concealment, selective disclosure, NGO safe spaces and private online groups. Some participants turned to substance use, and they linked concealment to depression and suicidal thoughts. The author recommends public education, more mental health services outside Harare, legal gender-marker change, gender-affirming healthcare, protection for intersex minors, and an end to hate speech.
