This bachelor thesis (Mendel University, 2025) asks why participants in foreign-funded development projects in Zambia seek assistance, what forms they want, and how they secure it. Using an ethnographic, qualitative design, the author held four focus groups and 27 interviews during a 2024 field trip. The 37 informants were farmers, beekeepers, tailors, cooperative members and NGO managers in the Western, Central and Southern regions, and the data were analysed thematically. Participants were mainly motivated by raising family income, starting small businesses and gaining training. Some women also cited empowerment, and others joined because of peer pressure. They favoured tangible support, such as pumps, machines, market access and skills, over unrestricted cash. Informants strategically adopted donor vocabulary (empowerment, SDGs), framed needs as projects, and appealed to vulnerability and long-term partnership. The author reads this as adaptive agency rather than passive dependency, although some actors treat assistance as a structural necessity and dependence varies even within communities. Limitations include non-generalizability, translation problems, multiple interviewers and the researcher’s positionality as a European academic linked to a donor-implementing university. The thesis recommends long-term, co-created partnerships that strengthen local autonomy.
