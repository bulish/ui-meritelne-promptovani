Summary

This master’s thesis examines how tax policy relates to economic growth in Ghana, using annual data for 1990–2022 from the Ghana Revenue Authority, the Ministry of Finance, and World Development Indicators. The variables are GDP growth, total tax revenue, capital expenditure, revenue (recurrent) expenditure, and inflation. The methods are ADF unit-root tests, VAR lag selection, Johansen cointegration, a Vector Error Correction Model (VECM), and Granger causality. All series are non-stationary at levels and stationary after first differencing. Cointegration is found among the variables. In the long-run equation, tax revenue and capital expenditure are positively linked to GDP, while inflation and revenue expenditure are negatively linked. In the GDP-based VECM, the error-correction term is negative and significant (about −0.043, roughly 4.3% adjustment per period), and lagged tax revenue is significant. Granger tests show one-way causality from total tax revenue to GDP, with no short-run causality for the other variables. The author recommends broadening the tax base, modernizing tax administration, prioritizing capital spending, and controlling the public wage bill. Limitations include omitted variables, structural breaks (the 2008 crisis, Covid-19), and the small sample.
