This diploma thesis asks whether publication bias shapes published estimates of cybercrime costs, which range from under $1 million per breach to trillions of dollars annually. The author compiled 20 publicly available studies from industry, government and academic sources, selected from over 40 candidates using four inclusion criteria (English, numerical estimate, stated sample size or method, original data). Each study was reduced to one cost figure, converted to USD, with ranges replaced by midpoints. Because standard errors were rarely available, 1/sample size served as a precision proxy. Rather than formal tests or regression, the author relies on a funnel plot and descriptive comparisons. The thesis reports that industry studies, typically with smaller or undisclosed samples, show higher estimates (above $4 million per breach) than academic or government ones, and that similar industry figures are repeated across reports. The author reads this as a small-study effect and selective visibility, with industry incentives as a plausible driver. The thesis itself stresses that the evidence is suggestive, not proof: the dataset is small, heterogeneous, lacks contextual variables, and funnel plots are descriptive. Recommendations stress transparency for policymakers, firms, researchers and media.

Caveat worth noting: the thesis’s own data presentation has weaknesses that make its conclusions shakier than the text suggests. For example, Table 5.5 shows five records, Figure 6.2 plots only about six points, and Figure 6.3’s government and academic groups contain a single observation each. The funnel plot’s axes (log cost on x, log 1/n on y) also differ from the conventional layout, and the text cites “Figure 6.1,” which does not appear. The author’s own hedging (“not statistical proof”) is therefore well warranted.
