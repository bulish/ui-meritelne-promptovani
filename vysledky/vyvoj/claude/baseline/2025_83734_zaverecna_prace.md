Summary

This diploma thesis compares how employed university students in South Korea (Pukyong National University, n = 141) and the Czech Republic (Mendel University in Brno, n = 132) experience work–study–life balance and how it relates to well-being. It uses a self-designed online questionnaire with five-point Likert items. The analysis in SPSS covers t-tests, Spearman correlations, regression and chi-square tests. Korean students studied more, while Czech students spent significantly more time on paid work and household duties. Both groups reported similar perceived time availability, ability to rest, and pressure on personal time. Czech students reported stronger health and relationship strain, higher work-related stress, more emotional exhaustion, and more willingness to seek counselling. Korean students reported higher academic, social-expectation and future-related stress, more anxiety, and stronger cultural and family pressure. Lack of personal time predicted emotional exhaustion in both groups. Czech students were significantly more satisfied with their balance. The author attributes the differences to competitive, Confucian-influenced academic culture versus European autonomy norms. She notes limits: two universities only, translation effects, and sensitive self-reported data.
