Summary (174 words)

This bachelor’s thesis examines the role of student movements in Thailand’s 2020–2021 pro-democracy protests through Social Movement Theory, using a qualitative single-case design based on secondary sources. The dissolution of the Future Forward Party in 2020 triggered youth-led, decentralized mobilization that relied on social media (Twitter, Facebook, Telegram), student and campus networks, creative cultural symbolism, small donations, and transnational links such as the Milk Tea Alliance. Within a largely closed, semi-authoritarian political opportunity structure, protesters framed grievances around military-backed rule, the 2017 constitution, and generational injustice, demanding the prime minister’s resignation, parliament’s dissolution, and a new constitution; they later broke a taboo by pressing for monarchy reform. The government responded mainly with prosecutions (including lèse-majesté), emergency powers, force, and digital controls, and most constitutional reform proposals were rejected. The movement reshaped public discourse, provoked royalist countermobilization and polarization, and fed into the Move Forward Party’s 2023 election win, yet structural constraints, including the party’s 2024 dissolution, limited lasting institutional change. The author notes limitations: reliance on secondary data, one case, and predefined categories.
