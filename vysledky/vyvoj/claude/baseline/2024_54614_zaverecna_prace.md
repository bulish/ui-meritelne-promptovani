Summary (about 185 words)

This dissertation asks whether pre-industrial wood-protection practices can be revived as non-toxic alternatives to modern chemical preservatives. A literature review covers historical methods, from air, water, fire and soil to ammonia fuming, blood and plant-derived substances. It notes that natural preservatives face barriers: regulatory registration, leaching, heterogeneity, and lab results that do not match field tests. Four experimental articles then test selected techniques.

One-sided charring of beech at 200–400 °C darkened the surface and lowered its density. It improved fire and water-related properties, mainly in radial specimens. Six minutes at 250 °C worked best, but tangential specimens varied more, and cracking limits exterior use. Ammonia fuming and iron-nanoparticle treatment darkened oak. Ammonia-treated wood lightened again under light, and iron-treated wood changed colour more, especially with higher extractive content. None of the treatments improved outdoor colour stability. Finally, linseed-oil paints with plant resins eroded and lost gloss and water repellence over time. Opaque coatings showed minimal colour change after six years and outlasted lazure ones, and layered plain linseed paint was highly effective. The author calls for further study.
