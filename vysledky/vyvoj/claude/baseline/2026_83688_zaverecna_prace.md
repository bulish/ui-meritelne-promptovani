Summary (175 words)

This bachelor thesis asks whether early retirement, proxied by statutory retirement age, affects the size of the shadow economy in seven Central European countries: Austria, Czechia, Germany, Hungary, Poland, Slovakia and Slovenia. Using annual World Bank and OECD data for 2010–2024, the author estimates a separate MIMIC (structural equation) model for each country in Stata. The shadow economy is a latent variable driven by GDP per capita, unemployment, self-employment, government effectiveness, taxes, social security contributions, retirement age and net migration. Retirement age had no significant effect everywhere except Hungary, where a higher women’s retirement age was associated with a smaller shadow economy, plausibly because of the “Women 40” scheme. The author attributes the null results to gradual, predictable reforms. Social security contributions were significant in six of seven countries and, unexpectedly, mostly negative. Self-employment was negative in Austria, Germany and Poland, and personal income tax was positive in Czechia, Hungary and Slovakia. Determinants vary by country, so retirement-age reforms alone are unlikely to curb informality. Limitations include simplified retirement-age data and sensitivity to model specification.
