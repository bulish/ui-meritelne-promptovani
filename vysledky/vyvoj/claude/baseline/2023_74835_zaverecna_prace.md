This diploma thesis examines how internally displaced persons (IDPs) from conflict-affected northern Ethiopia use smartphones. Data came from 300 one-to-one interviews, conducted in Amharic and Tigrigna in Addis Ababa and several Amhara towns, using chain-referral sampling and a structured questionnaire. Analysis combined descriptive statistics and correlations in Python with decision tree and random forest models.

Respondents were mostly uneducated (54%) and largely jobless after displacement (84%), yet 68% reported rising phone use since migrating. Phones were used mainly for calls and SMS (99%), internet access (72%), pictures and music. Seventy-two percent had social media accounts, with Facebook, Telegram and WhatsApp most common, mainly for contacting family, getting information and entertainment. About 57% used mobile phones to send or receive money. Having an income was associated with increased use, while staying over a year was associated with lower growth in use and with less social media use. Main barriers were poor internet connection, electricity shortages and device cost. Both models reached roughly 87-89% accuracy in predicting reasons for use and favored apps; tuning did not improve results. The author recommends larger samples, nationwide research, digital literacy programs and affordable devices and internet access.
