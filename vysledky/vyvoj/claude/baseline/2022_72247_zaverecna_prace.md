This diploma thesis compares Israel’s international legal obligations toward Palestinian children in the West Bank with what happens in practice. It first explains that Palestinians are tried in Israeli military courts, while Israeli settlers in the same territory fall under civilian law, giving minors markedly different protections. It then reviews nine instruments Israel has ratified, including the Fourth Geneva Convention, the Convention on the Rights of the Child, the ICCPR and the Convention Against Torture. Israel disputes their application in the occupied territories, but the ICJ and UN bodies reject that position.

The author relies mainly on hundreds of sworn testimonies gathered by Defense for Children International–Palestine, supported by UNICEF, B’Tselem and soldier accounts. They describe frequent night arrests, restraint and blindfolding, physical and verbal abuse, interrogation without a lawyer or parent, solitary confinement, very high confession and plea rates, transfer to prisons inside Israel, and psychological and educational harm, with almost no accountability. Reforms such as the juvenile military court and amended military orders are assessed, but critics like Viterbo call them tokenistic because “security offense” exceptions exclude most cases. The author remains sceptical that protection will improve.
