Goal
The thesis assesses taxation as a catalyst for economic growth in Ghana. It examines the association between taxation and growth, the link between capital expenditure and growth, and the link between revenue expenditure and growth.

Methods
The study used annual time-series data for 1990–2022 from the Ghana Revenue Authority, the Ministry of Finance and the World Development Indicators. Variables were GDP growth, total tax revenue, capital expenditure, revenue expenditure and inflation. Analysis combined stationarity testing, Johansen cointegration, a Vector Error Correction Model (VECM) and Granger causality.

Main results
All variables had unit roots at levels and were stationary after first differencing, I(1). Johansen tests supported cointegration up to rank 3. The long-run equation gave coefficients of -0.122 (inflation), 0.205 (capital expenditure), -0.091 (revenue expenditure) and 0.020 (total tax revenue). In the GDP VECM, the error correction term was -0.043 (p = 0.023), indicating about 4.3% adjustment per period. Lagged total tax revenue was significant (0.079, p = 0.016), while inflation, capital expenditure and revenue expenditure were not. Granger tests showed total tax revenue Granger-causing GDP (F = 3.905, p = 0.037), with no reverse causality. No Granger relationship was found among GDP, inflation, capital expenditure and revenue expenditure.

Conclusions
Taxes and growth have a statistically significant positive causal link. The author recommends stronger revenue mobilisation (broader tax base, modernised customs, rationalised exemptions), prioritising capital spending with monitoring, and managing the public wage bill.
