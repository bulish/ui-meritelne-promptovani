Goal
The thesis examines Israel’s obligations under international law toward Palestinian children in the West Bank, compares them with the reality children face in the Israeli military justice system, and reviews recent reforms.

Methods
A qualitative and quantitative case study covering the Second Intifada to the present. It reviews nine legal texts and draws mainly on sworn testimonies collected by Defense for Children International – Palestine (DCI-P), cross-checked with UNICEF, B’Tselem, Israeli NGOs and Israeli authority data, plus soldier testimonies and scholarly articles.

Main results

DCI-P’s 311 testimonies (2008–2012): 60% arrested between midnight and 5am; 95% restrained; 30% reported violence during arrest; none interrogated with a lawyer present and 99.4% without a parent; 12% held in solitary confinement (average 11 days); 58% confessed.
DCI-P’s 429 testimonies (2012–2015): 41.7% arrested at night; 97.7% restrained; 66.7% reported physical violence during arrest; 88.1% did not know the reason for arrest; 15% held in solitary (average 13 days), 90.9% of whom confessed; 99.3% pleaded guilty; 13.5% released on bail.
The military courts reported a 99.74% conviction rate in 2010.
In 2013, 654 minors were arrested; 465 (71%) were indicted.
Of 35 complaints DCI-P lawyers filed (2012–2015), none led to an indictment.
Reforms (juvenile court 2009, age of majority raised to 18, shorter detention before court, recording of interrogations) exclude security offences such as stone throwing.

Conclusions
The author concludes that abuse is systematic and that reforms yield little real progress because of exemption clauses, unaddressed abuse before detention, and gaps between procedure and practice. The author remains sceptical about Israeli reform, stresses the lack of post-release support, and places hope in grassroots initiatives on the Palestinian side.
