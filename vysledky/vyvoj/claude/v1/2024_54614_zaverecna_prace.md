Goal
To review traditional wood preservation methods used before the first Industrial Revolution and evaluate their effectiveness scientifically, including charred wood, ammonia-fumed wood colour, and oil-resin paints.

Methods
Literature review of historical methods (air, water, fire, soil, ammonia fuming, blood, plants), complemented by four experimental articles on charred beech, ammonia-treated oak, and linseed-oil paints.

Main results
Kiln drying was fastest, whereas air drying gave the best quality with fewer defects. Pond storage was abandoned about 50 years ago; storage over a year causes collapse and ring failure and harms water quality. Graham and Miller (1957) judged charred fence posts ineffective compared with now-banned preservatives; a 2019 experiment showed protective potential but high gas consumption. Blood coatings did not resist wood-decaying fungi and seemed to promote fungal growth; termites all died, attributed to the coating’s higher pH; fire resistance was not proven. Linseed oil typically penetrates 1.5–3 mm; pigment-stained paint on pine averaged 0.2 mm. Black locust was most susceptible to ammonia colouring.

Conclusions
Natural preservatives are antimicrobial, renewable and environmentally friendly, but heterogeneity, leaching, biodegradation and registration conflicts limit adoption. Combining them with other substances may help, and further research is needed.
