Goal
To explore how LGBTQ people in Zimbabwe experience discrimination, how societal norms, religion and law shape their lives, and how they cope.

Methods
Qualitative interpretive study framed by Minority Stress Theory. Snowball sampling, seeded via GALZ and PAKASIPITI, yielded 18 participants aged 18–35 (6 lesbian, 5 gay, 3 transgender, 4 intersex). Each took part in a 15–30-minute semi-structured interview via WhatsApp or Microsoft Teams. Data were analysed thematically and through narrative inquiry.

Main results
Six participants had been outed and three came out voluntarily; the rest remained closeted. Disclosure brought family disownment, forced marriage and exclusion. All participants reported verbal abuse. Workplace discrimination was widespread: lesbian footballers concealed their sexuality to keep team places, though one transgender woman found inclusive employers. Churches were mostly hostile. Gay men reported police detention and extortion (one was asked for US$300). Lesbians feared corrective rape. Transgender participants could not change identity documents, which led to a denied border crossing and a job refusal. Concealment was the most prevalent coping strategy. Others were selective disclosure, safe spaces (GALZ, PAKASIPITI, TRANSSMART), private WhatsApp groups and substance use.

Conclusions
Laws, religion and culture sustain stigma, and concealment fuels depression and substance abuse. The author recommends sensitization, mental-health services beyond Harare, legal gender-marker change and ending hate speech.
