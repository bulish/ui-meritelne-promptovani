Summary

Goal
To explore how actors in foreign-funded development projects in Zambia depend on development assistance, examining their motivations, the forms of assistance they seek, and the strategies they use to secure support.

Methods
Qualitative, ethnographic study based on fieldwork in autumn 2024: 27 individual interviews and four focus groups with 37 informants (11 male, 26 female), including farmers, beekeepers, tailors, managers and NGO staff across three Zambian regions, analysed thematically.

Main results
Informants were motivated chiefly by improving their own or their family’s income, with education, empowerment and social influence also mentioned. They preferred tangible assistance (agricultural equipment, market access, training) over unrestricted financial aid. Requests were framed in donor language (“empowerment”, SDGs), project formats and vulnerability narratives, or as appeals to partnership and moral obligation. Dependency was not uniform: managers described some farmers as dependent and others as self-sufficient.

Conclusions
Many actors treat external assistance as a structural necessity rather than a transitional tool. Their strategic adaptation, though rational, entrenches dependency. The findings cannot be generalized. The author recommends that donors invest in equitable, long-term partnerships that strengthen local autonomy and accountability, and that recipients be supported in developing self-sustaining strategies.
