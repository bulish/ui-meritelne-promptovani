Goal
To examine how employed university students in South Korea and the Czech Republic perceive the balance between study, work and personal life, and how this balance relates to their well-being.

Methods
A comparative cross-sectional survey. Working students at Pukyong National University and Mendel University in Brno completed an online questionnaire built on five-point Likert scales. Responses were analysed with t-tests, Spearman correlations, regression and chi-square tests.

Main results
Korean students studied more. Czech students spent more time on work, personal time and household duties. Sleep did not differ, and perceived time availability and ability to rest were similar in both groups. Czech students reported greater health and relationship strain, more work stress, more emotional exhaustion and more inability to cope. Koreans reported higher academic, social-expectation and future-uncertainty stress, and more anxiety/overload. Lack of personal time most strongly predicted exhaustion (CZ β = 0.495; KR β = 0.343). Mental-health service use differed between the groups (χ² = 40.300, p < .001). Koreans perceived stronger family influence (M = 3.95 vs. 2.83). Czech students were more satisfied with their work–study–life balance (M = 2.75 vs. 2.47; p = .018; d = 0.295).

Conclusions
Both groups face significant challenges. Student well-being emerges from the interaction of academic structures, employment conditions, cultural norms and individual coping capacities rather than from any single factor.
