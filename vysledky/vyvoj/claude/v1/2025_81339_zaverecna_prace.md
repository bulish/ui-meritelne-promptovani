Goal
The thesis examines whether publicly available cybercrime cost estimates show patterns suggesting publication bias, without claiming causality. It tests three hypotheses: industry reports give higher estimates (H1), smaller studies give higher estimates (H2), and “cost per breach” estimates vary more than “annual total” estimates (H3).

Methods
The author manually compiled 20 English-language studies (2011–2025) from academic, government, and industry sources, narrowed from over 40 candidates. Costs were converted to USD, ranges to midpoints, and 1/sample size served as a precision proxy. The analysis is descriptive and visual: a funnel plot and comparisons by source type.

Main results
Estimates range from under $1 million per incident to over $10 trillion annually. Industry studies average above $4 million per breach, often with modest or unreported samples. In the five-record sample, IBM (2021) reports $4.24M (n=503), Ponemon (2023) $4.45M (n=509), Kaspersky (2019) $3.80M (n=430), FBI IC3 (2020) $2.10M (n=550), and Anderson et al. (2012) $1.26M (n=300). The funnel plot shows high-cost, small-sample industry estimates, with academic and government studies nearer the center. Industry figures were also similar to one another. The author calls this suggestive, not statistical proof.

Conclusions
Patterns are consistent with selective reporting, though the small dataset, heterogeneous definitions, missing contextual variables, and visual-only method limit the findings. The author recommends methodological transparency, funding disclosure, and critical use of headline figures by policymakers, firms, researchers, and journalists.
