Summary

Goal
This bachelor’s thesis traces how Czech foreign policy toward the Srebrenica genocide evolved from 1995 to 2025. It also identifies the historical, political and international factors behind that evolution and assesses how far Czech policy aligns with the EU’s.

Methods
The study is a qualitative case study based on secondary analysis of official statements, UN and EU documents and media sources. It adds three semi-structured expert interviews, fieldwork in Bosnia and Herzegovina (March–April 2025), and a comparative analysis of Czech and EU discourse across seven categories.

Main results
Czech policy moved from initial uncertainty and partial denial (1995–1999) to firm commitment. In 1999 Czechia supported NATO’s Balkan intervention. In 2005 it officially recognized the genocide, four years before the EU’s formal recognition in 2009. In 2024 it supported the UN General Assembly resolution commemorating the genocide. Key influences were Czechia’s totalitarian experience, the Havel and Masaryk legacies, and ties to the UN, NATO and EU. President Zeman skipped the 2015 commemoration. Czech and EU positions are strongly aligned from the mid-2000s onward, including support for the ICTY and ICC. The EU has more institutionalized education and prevention tools (Erasmus+, Genocide Network), whereas Czech efforts are more ad hoc.

Conclusions
Czechia moved from caution to a consistent, principled position aligned with EU norms. The authors conclude that a small post-communist state can contribute meaningfully to justice and memory, though political ambivalence reveals tensions between values and domestic politics.
