Goal
The thesis compares income inequality across the EU-27 from 2007 to 2023 using three measures. It aims to identify trends and see where the measures agree or differ.

Methods
The study uses Eurostat’s disposable income decile shares. Croatia’s pre-2010 data come from the Croatian Bureau of Statistics. Inequality is computed in R with the Gini coefficient and Lorenz curves, the Atkinson index (ε = 0.5, 1, 2) and Generalized Entropy indices (α = 0, 1, 2).

Main results
The EU Gini was nearly unchanged (0.291 in 2007, 0.288 in 2023), which masks national divergence. Romania’s Gini fell from 0.375 to 0.305 and Poland’s from 0.315 to 0.265. Slovakia had the lowest 2023 Gini (0.215). Sweden rose from 0.229 to 0.286 and Malta from 0.258 to 0.321. Romania’s top decile lost about 6.3 percentage points of income share, while Malta’s gained 5.3. Sweden’s rise was +25.1% in Gini, +61.3% in A(0.5) and +65.7% in GE(2.0). Poland’s Gini fell 15.6% and GE(2.0) fell 32.9%. Bulgaria’s GE rose from 0.222 (α = 1) to 0.265 (α = 2). Correlations among measures in 2023 ranged from 0.8660 to 0.9998.

Conclusions
The stable EU average hides improvements in some states and setbacks in others. Using several measures shows where inequality is concentrated. The authors recommend policies suited to each country, such as stronger redistribution, education and inclusive labor markets.
