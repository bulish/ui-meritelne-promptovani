Summary

Goal: The dissertation reviews traditional wood preservation methods used from the beginning of human wood use up to the first Industrial Revolution and evaluates their effectiveness. It also sets experimental objectives on charred wood, ammonia-treated wood, and oil paints with plant resins.

Methods: A literature review groups methods into basic ones (air, water, fire, soil) and advanced ones (ammonia fuming, blood, plant-derived substances such as extractives, phenolics, isoprenoids, fatty acids, and alkaloids).

Main results: Air drying gave the best quality with fewer defects but took longer than kiln or solar drying. Water storage was abandoned about 50 years ago and replaced by sprinkling, partly because of its negative impact on water quality. Graham and Miller (1957) judged charred fence posts inefficient compared with now-banned preservatives. Ammonia treatment improved color stability. Blood coatings did not show fungal resistance and seemed to promote fungal growth, termites died because of the coating’s higher pH, and fire resistance was not experimentally proven. Linseed oil typically penetrates 1.5–3 mm.

Conclusions: Natural compounds offer antimicrobial properties and environmental friendliness but face heterogeneity, leaching, biodegradation, and registration barriers. Modern coatings often outlast historical treatments. Further research is needed.
