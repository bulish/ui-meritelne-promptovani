Summary

This diploma thesis examines the status of Palestinian children in the West Bank from the Second Intifada to the present, contrasting Israel’s international law obligations with practice on the ground. It asks what obligations Israel has towards Palestinian children and what reality they face. Methodologically, it is a case study that reviews nine legal texts (including the Fourth Geneva Convention, the Rome Statute, the UNCRC, and the ICCPR) and relies mainly on sworn testimonies collected by Defense for Children International – Palestine (DCI-P): 311 (2008–2012) and 429 (2012–2015). Results show a pattern of rights violations. Between 2008 and 2012, 60% of minors were arrested between midnight and 5am, 95% were restrained, 99.4% were interrogated without a parent, and none had a lawyer present. Between 2012 and 2015, 66.7% reported physical violence during arrest, and 99.3% pleaded guilty. The conviction rate reached 99.74% in 2010. Detainees’ transfer to Israel conflicts with Article 76 of the Fourth Geneva Convention. Reforms, including the 2009 juvenile court and Military Orders 1676, 1711, 1726, 1727 and 1745, exclude security offenses through exception clauses. The author concludes that real progress is doubtful, citing reforms criticised as “token” (Viterbo), unaddressed pre-detention abuse, and weak post-release support.
