Summary

Goal: The dissertation aims to define digital sovereignty scientifically for EU states, measure it quantitatively, and derive recommendations for strengthening it. It addresses three research gaps: no uniform definition, no measurement, and no evaluation of the effectiveness of fields of action (FoA).

Methods: The author synthesised a qualitative definition from a literature review (Foucaultian “good governance”, key enabling technologies, weak data sovereignty) and conducted structured interviews with five experts. A three-sub-index model (state sovereignty, focus on KETs, technological sovereignty) was quantified with 30 secondary-data parameters for 212 countries. The Digital Sovereignty Index (DSI) was built following OECD guidance, tested via permutations of normalisation, imputation, and weighting, and verified through a 1,000-run stress test and a 150-period simulation.

Main results: Equal weighting, raw data, and mean imputation performed best. The USA, Ireland, and Norway lead; all EU-27 states rank in the top 50. The DSI correlates with FSI (R² 0.86), BLI (0.77), and SDG ranks (0.71). EU states split into Groups A, B, and C, each with distinct FoA. Treated as one state, the EU would rank 4th.

Conclusions: The DSI is verifiable, valid, and robust. Group-specific FoA and a joint EU-level digital policy are recommended, though the author notes limitations such as parameter selection and imputation.
