Summary

This bachelor’s thesis aims to map the role of student movements in Thailand’s 2020–2021 pro-democracy protests, addressing three research questions on how students mobilized and formulated demands, how the government responded, and how the movements were reflected in political discourse. Methods: a qualitative, interpretative single-case study applying Social Movement Theory (resource mobilization, political opportunity structure, framing) to secondary sources (academic literature, media, NGO reports), coded by category. Main results: the movement mobilized through a decentralized, digitally coordinated structure (Twitter, Facebook, Telegram), student networks, creative symbols (the three-finger salute) and small-scale donations. It framed demands diagnostically, prognostically and motivationally, expanding from three core demands to a ten-point monarchy reform manifesto. The government responded mainly with emergency powers, lèse-majesté prosecutions, and force (water cannons, tear gas, rubber bullets). Constitutional amendments were rejected in March 2021. In 2023, the Move Forward Party won 151 of 500 seats but could not form a government and was dissolved in 2024. Conclusions: the movement strongly shaped mobilization, public discourse and electoral behavior, yet its institutional impact stayed limited under a resistant political system.
