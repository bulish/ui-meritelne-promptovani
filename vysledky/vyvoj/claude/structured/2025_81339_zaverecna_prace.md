Summary

This thesis investigates whether publication bias appears in published estimates of cybercrime costs, looking for signals rather than claiming causality. It tests three hypotheses: industry-sponsored reports give higher estimates (H1), smaller studies give higher estimates (H2), and “cost per breach” estimates show greater variance than “annual total” ones (H3). The author manually compiled 20 studies from academic, government and industry sources, narrowed from over 40 sources using four inclusion criteria. Costs were converted to USD using World Bank rates, ranges were reduced to midpoints, and 1/n served as a precision proxy. Results were evaluated through a funnel plot and descriptive comparison. Industry studies on average reported over $4 million per breach, often with modest or unreported samples, while Anderson et al. (2012) reported the lowest figure ($1.26M in the sample table). The funnel plot shows high costs concentrated in small industry studies, consistent with a “small-study effect.” The author concludes that patterns consistent with selective reporting exist, but that these do not prove bias. Limitations include the small dataset, heterogeneous definitions, missing contextual variables, and the descriptive nature of funnel plots. The thesis recommends methodological transparency for firms, policymakers and media.
