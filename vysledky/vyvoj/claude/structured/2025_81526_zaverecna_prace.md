Summary

Goal: This bachelor thesis examines how diverse actors in Zambian development projects have or have not become dependent on foreign development assistance. It asks what motivates them to participate, which forms of assistance they seek, and what strategies they use to secure external support.

Methods: A qualitative, ethnographic design was used during a 2024 academic field trip, with purposive and theoretical sampling. Data comprised four focus groups and twenty-seven individual interviews, plus participant observation. The sample included 37 informants (11 males, 26 females) across Mongu, Kashitu and Livingstone, analysed thematically.

Main results: Motivations were mainly economic, but also included education, empowerment, and social pressure. Informants preferred tangible help (machinery, pumps, pesticides, market access, training) over general funds. They framed requests using donor language such as “empowerment” and the SDGs, and appealed to partnership and vulnerability. Dependency was uneven among farmers.

Conclusions: Actors are strategic agents, not passive recipients. Yet many treat assistance as a structural necessity, with limited emphasis on internal revenue. The author calls for equitable, long-term partnerships strengthening local autonomy. Findings are not generalizable.
