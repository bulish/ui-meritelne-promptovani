Goal
To examine whether published cybercrime cost estimates show patterns consistent with publication bias, particularly whether smaller or industry-sponsored studies report higher estimates.
Methods
The thesis used a simplified meta-analytical approach based on 20 publicly available studies from academic, government, and industry sources, covering publications from 2011 to 2025. Reported costs and sample sizes were standardized and analyzed descriptively using a funnel plot, with 1/n as a proxy for study precision. The analysis was visual rather than inferential and did not test causality.
Main results
Reported cybercrime costs ranged from under $1 million per incident to predictions exceeding $10 trillion annually. Industry studies generally reported higher estimates than academic and government sources. The funnel plot showed a concentration of high-cost estimates among relatively small studies, particularly industry sources, while academic and government studies clustered closer to the center with lower estimates and larger samples. Industry studies averaged exceeding $4 million per breach and often had modest or unreported sample sizes. Three recurring patterns emerged: higher estimates in smaller or undisclosed samples, higher estimates from industry sources, and clustering of similar high-impact industry figures.
Conclusions
The findings suggest patterns consistent with publication bias but do not conclusively prove bias or manipulation. The thesis recommends greater methodological transparency, disclosure of funding and sample sizes, standardized reporting, independent research, and more critical verification by policymakers and media.     2025_81339_zaverecna_prace
