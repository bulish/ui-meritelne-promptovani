Goal
The thesis examines how diverse actors in development projects in Zambia depend on foreign development assistance, focusing on their motivations, the forms of assistance they seek, and strategies used to secure external support.     2025_81526_zaverecna_prace
Methods
The study used a qualitative ethnographic approach. Data were collected during an academic field trip to Zambia in autumn 2024 through purposive and theoretical sampling. The sample comprised 37 informants; data included four focus groups and 27 individual interviews and were analysed using thematic analysis.     2025_81526_zaverecna_prace     2025_81526_zaverecna_prace
Main results
Participants were predominantly motivated by pragmatic needs, especially improving their or their families’ economic situation, institutional continuity, capacity-building, and service delivery. They favoured tangible and immediate assistance, including financial support for operational costs, equipment, infrastructure, agricultural tools, market access, and training, rather than abstract long-term reforms. Participants also strategically aligned their needs and project language with donor priorities and cultivated foreign-partner networks. Limited emphasis on internal revenue generation and policy advocacy indicated continued reliance on donor funding, which further entrenched dependency and weakened incentives for autonomous development planning.     2025_81526_zaverecna_prace     2025_81526_zaverecna_prace(1)     2025_81526_zaverecna_prace(1)
Conclusions
The authors conclude that many actors treat external assistance as a structural necessity rather than a transitional tool. They recommend equitable, long-term partnerships that strengthen local autonomy and accountability, alongside self-sustaining strategies rooted in local agency and ownership.     2025_81526_zaverecna_prace
