**Goal:** The thesis examines the role of student movements in Thailand’s 2020–2021 pro-democracy protests, focusing on mobilization, demand formation, governmental responses, political discourse, and longer-term impact.

**Methods:** It uses a qualitative, interpretative single-case study guided by Social Movement Theory (SMT). The analysis applies resource mobilization, political opportunity structure, and framing to secondary data from academic literature, media, NGOs, and human-rights watchdogs, comparing findings with the 2023 election.

**Main results:** Students mobilized through Facebook, Twitter, Telegram, Line, decentralized coordination, culturally relatable symbols, educational institutions, donations, crowdfunding, and transnational networks. Framing used diagnostic, prognostic, and motivational processes and expanded demands from constitutional and governmental reform to the sensitive role of the monarchy, broadening political debate. Government responses were primarily repressive, including arrests, legal prosecution, assembly restrictions, surveillance, and force; limited reform discussions produced no significant institutional change. The movement influenced political discourse and electoral behavior: the MFP won 151 of 500 House seats in 2023; turnout exceeded 75%, with nearly 40 million votes and around five million first-time voters.

**Conclusions:** Student movements shaped mobilization, discourse, and electoral politics, but achieved limited immediate institutional change. Their influence persisted through political participation and reform agendas, while repression and resistant institutions constrained structural change.
