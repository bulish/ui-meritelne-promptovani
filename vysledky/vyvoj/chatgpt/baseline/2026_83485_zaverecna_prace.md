The study examines the lived experiences of LGBTQ+ people in Zimbabwe, focusing on discrimination, social and cultural attitudes, legal constraints, coping strategies, and support systems. Using a qualitative design, the researcher conducted 18 in-depth interviews with participants aged 18–35, recruited mainly through snowball sampling and LGBTQ+ organizations.

The findings show that participants experience interconnected forms of stigma in workplaces, families, religious communities, and public life. Workplace discrimination, fear of dismissal, and pressure to conceal sexual orientation were particularly prominent. Religious and cultural beliefs frequently portrayed LGBTQ+ identities as sinful, un-African, or unacceptable, although some participants resisted shame and maintained strong self-acceptance. Disclosure could lead to family rejection, homelessness, forced heterosexual marriage, blackmail, and extortion. Participants commonly coped through concealment, selective disclosure, private social networks, and LGBTQ+ safe spaces. These networks provided companionship, psychological support, rights education, and a sense of belonging, demonstrating resilience despite persistent structural and interpersonal pressures.
