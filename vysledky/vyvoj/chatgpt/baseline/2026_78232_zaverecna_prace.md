Summary (150–200 words)

The thesis examines whether forest biomass used for bioenergy can contribute to climate-change mitigation while remaining environmentally and economically sustainable. A scoping review of 39 studies found eight different concepts of carbon neutrality, showing that carbon neutrality is not an inherent property of biomass but depends on feedstock origin, temporal and spatial boundaries, and counterfactual scenarios. The thesis therefore recommends life-cycle assessment (LCA) as a comprehensive framework for evaluating biomass-related emissions.

An LCA of residential heat production from sawmill-residue pellets in Northwest Russia found relatively low environmental impacts within the defined system. Pellet combustion and waste disposal were the largest contributors to five of eight impact categories, while pellet production dominated global warming, marine eutrophication, and fossil-resource scarcity. Longer transport distances substantially increased impacts, demonstrating the importance of supply-chain design.

A second scoping review identified more than 20 monetary ecosystem-service valuation approaches, but found inconsistent terminology and methodological hybridization. Overall, the thesis concludes that forest biomass can support climate mitigation, but its sustainability requires transparent LCA, optimized supply chains, and standardized economic valuation methods.
