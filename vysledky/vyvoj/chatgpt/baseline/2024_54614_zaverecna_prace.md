Summary
The dissertation investigates historical and traditional approaches to wood preservation through four experimental studies. The first two examine one-sided surface charring of beech wood under different time–temperature conditions. Charring substantially altered surface colour and roughness, with the effects depending on the anatomical orientation of the wood. It also modified carbohydrates beneath the surface and improved resistance to fungal decay; radial specimens generally showed more consistent behaviour than tangential ones. The second study additionally demonstrates that charring changes the density profile and surface wettability, indicating that treatment conditions are important for obtaining desirable properties.  
The third study examines oak treated with ammonia gas or iron nanoparticles. Both treatments produced substantial colour changes, while the extent of ammonia-induced discoloration was strongly related to extractive content. During accelerated ageing, all specimens underwent photodegradation, but ammonia-treated wood showed comparatively better colour stability under dry conditions.  
The fourth study evaluates historical linseed-oil paints containing plant resins after six years of outdoor exposure. Opaque coatings showed relatively little colour change, whereas lazure coatings exhibited greater variation in measured properties. Overall, the studies demonstrate that historical treatments can significantly modify wood surfaces, but their effects depend strongly on treatment conditions and wood structure.
