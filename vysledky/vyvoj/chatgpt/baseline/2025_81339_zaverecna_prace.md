Summary

The thesis investigates whether publication bias shapes widely reported estimates of cybercrime costs. Rather than estimating a single “true” cost, it examines whether published figures show systematic patterns associated with study size and source type. The author manually compiled 20 publicly available industry, governmental, and academic studies and standardized their reported costs and sample sizes. A funnel plot, using inverse sample size as a proxy for precision, was then used to identify potential asymmetry. The analysis found that smaller or undisclosed-sample studies tended to report higher costs, while industry-sponsored reports generally produced higher estimates than academic and governmental sources. High-impact industry figures were also repeatedly cited and disseminated, potentially increasing their visibility relative to more conservative estimates. These patterns are presented as evidence consistent with publication bias, not proof of intentional manipulation or causality. The thesis recommends greater methodological transparency, standardized reporting, independent research, and more critical treatment of dramatic cost figures by policymakers, firms, researchers, and journalists. Its conclusions are constrained by the small 20-study dataset, heterogeneous measures, missing contextual variables, and the descriptive nature of funnel-plot analysis.
