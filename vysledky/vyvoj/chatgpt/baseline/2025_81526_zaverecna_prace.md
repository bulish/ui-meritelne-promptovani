Summary

This thesis examines how local actors in Zambia engage with and depend on foreign development assistance, focusing on their motivations, preferred forms of support, and strategies for obtaining it. Using an ethnographic qualitative approach, the study draws on 27 individual interviews, four focus groups, and observations involving 37 informants across several Zambian regions.

The findings show that participation is primarily motivated by practical economic needs, including income generation, employment, equipment, training, and improved family welfare. Participants generally prefer tangible assistance—especially agricultural equipment, market access, and capacity building—over abstract long-term interventions. Local actors also strategically adapt their language and proposals to donor priorities, using concepts such as “empowerment,” “sustainability,” and the SDGs to attract support.

The study argues that dependency is not uniform: some participants develop greater autonomy, while others remain strongly reliant on external resources. Overall, assistance is portrayed as a negotiated relationship in which recipients exercise agency while operating within structures that can reinforce dependency. The thesis therefore calls for long-term, locally owned partnerships that strengthen autonomy, capacity, and accountability.
