The 2020–2021 pro-democracy protests in Thailand were a major episode of youth-led political mobilization that challenged both the military-backed government and established taboos surrounding the monarchy. The thesis argues that student movements were central to the protests because they combined decentralized organization with extensive digital mobilization. Facebook, Twitter, Telegram, and LINE enabled rapid coordination, information sharing, collective identity-building, and continued participation despite repression. Students also drew on universities, diverse participant skills, crowdfunding, and transnational networks such as the Milk Tea Alliance.
Using Social Movement Theory, the thesis shows that protesters formulated demands through diagnostic, prognostic, and motivational framing. Initial demands focused on the prime minister’s resignation, parliamentary dissolution, and constitutional reform, later expanding to questions concerning the monarchy, democracy, equality, and generational justice. The government responded primarily through arrests, legal prosecution, emergency powers, surveillance, and physical force rather than substantial reform. Although institutional change remained limited, the movement significantly broadened political debate and influenced electoral politics.
