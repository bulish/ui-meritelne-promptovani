The study examines whether early retirement affects the size of the shadow economy in seven Central European countries—Austria, Czechia, Germany, Hungary, Poland, Slovakia, and Slovenia—over 2010–2024. Using a MIMIC-based analysis, it finds that retirement age has no statistically significant effect on the shadow economy in any country except Hungary. There, women’s retirement age is negatively associated with shadow-economy size, possibly reflecting Hungary’s specific pension reforms and the “Women 40” scheme. The study therefore concludes that pension parameters alone are insufficient to explain changes in informal economic activity. Instead, the results point to substantial country-specific variation and emphasize tax, social-protection, labour-market, and institutional factors. Social security contributions were statistically significant in all countries except Slovakia and generally showed an inverse relationship with the shadow economy. Self-employment also had an inverse effect in Germany, Austria, and Poland, where it is largely formalized. Other determinants—including personal income tax, unemployment, GDP per capita, migration, government effectiveness, and corporate taxation—showed weaker or more heterogeneous effects. Overall, the study argues that reducing the shadow economy requires comprehensive policies adapted to national institutional conditions rather than uniform changes to retirement age.
