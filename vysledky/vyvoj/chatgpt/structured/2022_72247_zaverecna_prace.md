1. Summary (184 words)
Goal: The thesis analyzes how international law affects Israel’s treatment of Palestinian minors in the West Bank, identifying Israel’s obligations and comparing them with the reality experienced by children, while also examining recent reforms to the military justice system.  
Methods: It uses a case-study approach combining qualitative and quantitative evidence. Nine international legal texts were reviewed, while evidence on conditions on the ground was primarily based on interviews conducted by Defense for Children International–Palestine (DCI-P), supported by signed affidavits and corroborated with data from Israeli authorities and other organizations.  
Main results: The thesis identifies numerous discrepancies between international-law protections and practice. Among 311 DCI-P testimonies from 2008–2012, 30% of minors reported physical violence during arrest; 99.4% were interrogated without a parent, and none had a lawyer present. Physical violence during interrogation was reported in 37% of cases, while 52% reported threats. In 2010, the conviction rate reached 99.74%; 90% pleaded guilty. Between 2012–2015, 35 complaints concerning ill-treatment and torture resulted in no indictments.  
Conclusions: The thesis considers recent reforms insufficient because exemption clauses, pre-detention abuse, and gaps between procedures and their application remain. It also identifies inadequate post-release educational and psychological support.
