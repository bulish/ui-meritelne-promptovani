1. Summary
Goal: The thesis examines the dependency of participants in development projects in Zambia on foreign development assistance, focusing on motivations for participation, the forms of assistance sought, and strategies used to secure external support. Methods: The study uses a qualitative, ethnographic design based on semi-structured interviews, focus groups, participant observation, and document analysis. Data were collected during an academic field trip to Zambia in autumn 2024, using purposive and theoretical sampling. The study included four focus groups, twenty-seven individual interviews, and 37 informants; responses were analysed using thematic analysis. Main results: Actors were predominantly motivated by pragmatic needs, including access to funding, equipment, training opportunities, and international recognition. Most sought financial support for operational costs, material resources such as equipment and infrastructure, and short-term capacity-building programs, favouring tangible and immediate assistance. Strategies included strategic alignment with donor priorities, adaptation of project language to donor expectations, and cultivating networks with foreign partners. Conclusions: The study finds that many actors engage with external assistance as a structural necessity, while strategic adaptation can reinforce dependency and weaken autonomous development planning. However, findings cannot be generalized across all Zambian contexts. The thesis calls for equitable, long-term partnerships strengthening local autonomy and accountability.     2025_81526_zaverecna_prace
