1. Summary (183 words)
Goal: This thesis examines whether publication bias is present in publicly available estimates of cybercrime costs, focusing on whether smaller studies and industry-sponsored reports tend to report higher estimates. It aims to identify patterns or signals of selective reporting rather than establish causality.  
Methods: The study manually compiled a dataset of 20 publicly available industry, academic, and governmental reports. Studies were selected using four inclusion criteria, including a numerical cybercrime-cost estimate, sample-size information, and original data. Seven variables were recorded, including sample_size, effect_value, effect_type, and bias_proxy, calculated as 1/sample_size. After data cleaning and currency conversion using World Bank average annual exchange rates, the analysis used a funnel plot with 1/n as the horizontal axis and reported cost as the vertical axis, supplemented by descriptive comparisons.  
Main results: Cost estimates ranged from under $1 million per incident to over $10 trillion annually. High-cost estimates were concentrated among relatively small, particularly industry, studies. Industry studies generally reported estimates exceeding $4 million per breach, while academic and government sources reported more conservative figures.  
Conclusions: The observed asymmetry is consistent with publication bias and a “small-study effect,” but does not constitute statistical proof. The thesis recommends greater methodological transparency and larger-scale research.
